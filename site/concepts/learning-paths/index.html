    Learning paths and curriculum¶

    A path is a route through a graph rather than a self-contained course, and the gap between those two things is what the reader pays for.

    core · difficulty 3 · 5h

    Asked for by name

    Intel: "structured learning paths"

    Prerequisites: Diátaxis, Instructional design for developers

    Leads to: Workshops and hands-on labs

    What it is¶

    An ordered sequence through existing content, aimed at one audience and one outcome. A path adds no new material. It makes two claims about material that already exists: that this is the right subset, and that this is the right order.

    Both claims are checkable, which is what separates a learning path from a list of links with a heading, and almost all published learning paths are lists of links with a heading.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because Diátaxis organises content by its kind, and a reader does not arrive with a kind. They arrive with a job.

    A corpus sorted into tutorials, how-to guides, reference and explanation is navigable and gives no answer to "I started on Monday, what do I read first". The four quadrants are an authoring discipline and a filing system; a path is the reading order that the filing system deliberately does not encode. Intel asks for "structured learning paths" next to golden paths and quickstarts because those are the three shapes of the same obligation at three scales: one command, one page, one career's first fortnight.

    The failure mode is specific and nearly universal: a path that is a table of contents, ordered by the order someone wrote the pages in, with no claim about prerequisites that anything verifies.

    The golden path¶

    Validate a real curriculum: nine paths over 106 concepts. Find that the ordering claim holds, the coverage claim does not, and the promise made at the top of every path page is false. About thirty-five minutes.

    Step 1: the paths as data, not as pages

    paths:
  - id: first-week
    name: The first week
    audience: Someone who has just taken one of these roles and needs to be useful by Friday.
    concepts: [technical-writing, docs-as-product, docs-as-code, markdown,
               git-authoring, diataxis, static-site-generators, mkdocs,
               link-checking, documentation-ci]

    A path is a list of concept ids. That is the whole structure, and it is the reason any of what follows is possible: the path page is generated, so a path cannot link to a concept that does not exist, and the sequence can be checked against the prerequisite graph.

      first-week             10 steps  34h
  content-architect      13 steps  43h
  docs-engineer          18 steps  70h
  knowledgeops           17 steps  83h
  developer-educator     15 steps  60h
  writer-who-builds      10 steps  37h
  prove-it               15 steps  59h

    Step 2: check the ordering claim

    Every path page says the order matters. For each step, every prerequisite appearing in the same path must appear earlier:

    for learning_path in reg.paths:
    pos = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(learning_path.concepts)}
    for concept in learning_path.concepts:
        for prerequisite in reg.by_id[concept].prerequisites:
            if prerequisite in pos and pos[prerequisite] > pos[concept]:
                findings.path_order_violations.append(...)

    learning paths that teach a concept before its prerequisite: 0

    Zero across nine paths and 114 steps, and not because the paths were written carefully. They were reordered twice to satisfy this check when it was first added. That is the check paying for itself: a curriculum nobody validates drifts out of order the first time a concept gains a prerequisite.

    Step 3: check the claim the validator deliberately does not make

    The validator contains this comment:

    # A prerequisite outside the path is fine: a path is a route
# through the graph, not a self-contained curriculum.
if prerequisite in learning_path.concepts and prerequisite not in seen:

    A defensible decision, and an unmeasured one. Count the pairs it permits:

      first-week           steps assuming something the path never teaches:  1
  content-architect                                                      1
  docs-engineer                                                          5
  knowledgeops                                                           3
  developer-educator                                                     4
  writer-who-builds                                                      3
  prove-it                                                              10

total step-prerequisite pairs pointing outside their path: 27

    Ten of prove-it's fifteen steps depend on something the path does not cover. A reader finishing it has been walked through fifteen concepts and quietly assumed to know seven more.

    Step 4: what the path page actually promised

    **15 concepts, roughly 59 hours.** The order matters:
each step assumes the ones above it.

    "Each step assumes the ones above it" is false for ten of those fifteen steps. Each step did disclose its own outside prerequisites further down the page, so the information was present and arrived after the reader had committed.

    This is the entry-state commitment from instructional design, applied to a path rather than a page, and it had been applied at step level and skipped at path level. The fix is to compute the assumed set and state it before step 1:

    assumed = []
for concept_id in learning_path.concepts:
    for prerequisite in by_id[concept_id].prerequisites:
        if prerequisite not in learning_path.concepts and prerequisite not in assumed:
            assumed.append(prerequisite)

    **Assumed before step 1.** This path does not teach Information architecture,
Choosing a static site generator, Git authoring workflows for writers, Style
guides and terminology management, Reproducible environments, Agent-ready
content, RAG pipelines. Each step below repeats what it assumes.

    Seven named concepts, before the hours are committed rather than after. A test asserts it appears above the first step, because the useful version of this information is the one the reader sees while deciding.

    Step 5: check the coverage claim, which is the worse number

    A path makes a claim about a subset. Ask what the subsets add up to:

    concepts reachable through some path: 84/106
concepts in no path at all:           22
   of those, written:                 22
   of those, core tier:                4

    Four core-tier concepts are in no path. Not thin pages: core means the curriculum asserts you cannot do the job without them, and every one of the twenty-two is written. They are reachable by search and by the dependency graph, and no curated route goes through them.

    That was eleven of thirty-seven when this measurement was first run, over seven paths. Two paths were added afterwards, one over measurement and automation and one over domain context, which is what moved it. The figures here are the current ones and fieldguide audit is where to read them; this page was wrong about them for a while, which is the hazard of a page whose subject is a measurement of the thing it lives in.

    Then the mirror image:

    concepts in exactly one path: 62
most reused: diataxis 4, docs-as-product 3, docs-as-code 3, link-checking 3

    Sixty-two concepts carry one path each. A path that is the sole route to well over half the corpus is doing navigation work, not curriculum work, and that is the honest reading of what nine paths over 106 concepts produces.

    Step 6: which of the two gaps to fix

    They want opposite things. Closing the prerequisite gaps means lengthening paths, and prove-it at 59 hours would become unrecommendable. Closing the coverage gap means adding paths or widening them, which worsens the prerequisite gaps.

    The resolution is to stop treating them as the same defect. Prerequisite gaps are a disclosure problem, fixed in step 4 at the cost of one computed paragraph. Coverage is a curriculum problem, and eleven uncovered core concepts is a decision about whether every core concept deserves a route, which is a judgment the numbers inform and do not make.

    What is not defensible is the state before this exercise: both gaps present, neither measured, and a promise at the top of every page that the data contradicted.

    What you built. Seven validated paths, an ordering check at zero violations, an entry state stated before step 1 and asserted by a test, and two measured gaps that pull in opposite directions.

    Reference¶

    Path against the alternatives¶

    Diátaxis quadrant Path Study plan
    Ordered by Kind of content One audience's job The dependency graph
    Covers Everything, filed A chosen subset Everything
    Written by The content model Judgment A topological sort
    Claims This is a how-to This subset, this order No cycles
    Fails by Miscategorising Omitting, or over-including Being 106 steps long

    This guide generates all three from one registry, which is the argument for keeping a path as data. The study plan is the honest fallback for the 37 concepts no path reaches.

    What a path has to declare¶

    Field Why
    Audience, in one sentence A path for everyone is a table of contents
    Outcome What the reader can do at the end
    Entry state See step 4; the one most often skipped
    Step order And a check that prerequisites precede their dependents
    Total time A reader commits hours, not steps
    Per-step time So a path can be paused

    How paths fail¶

    Failure Detected by
    A step precedes its prerequisite The ordering check
    Steps assume material the path omits The closure count, step 3
    The entry state is stated only per step Reading the top of the page
    Core concepts in no path The coverage count, step 5
    A path is the sole route to a third of the corpus The reuse count
    Links to pages that do not exist Generating the page from the registry
    Ordered by authoring date Nothing; this needs a person

    The last row is the common case in the wild, and no check catches it, because authoring order is a valid order. It is just never the right one.

    Sizing¶

    Length Reads as Risk
    Under 5 steps A reading list Not worth the page
    8 to 15 steps A path Right for most audiences
    Over 18 steps A syllabus Abandoned partway, invisibly
    Over 60 hours A qualification Needs checkpoints and a reason

    This guide's longest is 18 steps and 83 hours, which is over the line, and the mitigation is that every step is independently useful. That is a weaker answer than splitting it.

    Keeping it honest¶

    Rule Because
    Paths live in the registry, not in prose Pages generated from data cannot link to nothing
    Ordering is checked in CI A new prerequisite silently invalidates an order
    The entry state is computed, not written Hand-written assumptions go stale
    Coverage is reported Orphaned core concepts are invisible otherwise
    Reuse is reported A concept in four paths is load-bearing

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with why Diátaxis is not enough. "Diátaxis sorts content by kind, and a reader does not arrive with a kind, they arrive with a job. The four quadrants are an authoring discipline; a path is the reading order the filing system deliberately does not encode. So I keep paths as data in the registry and generate the pages, which means a path cannot point at a concept that does not exist, and the order can be checked against the prerequisite graph."

    The follow-up is what the check actually caught, and here the numbers are worth stating precisely. Ordering came out at zero violations across nine paths and 114 steps, and only because two paths were reordered when the check was added. The interesting number is the one the validator deliberately does not check: 32 step-prerequisite pairs point outside their path, and on prove-it ten of fifteen steps depend on something it never teaches.

    Then the finding that changed the generator. Every path page promised that each step assumes the ones above it, which was false for those ten steps. The information existed, disclosed per step, further down the page, after the reader had already committed to 59 hours. So the generator now computes the assumed set and states it before step 1, and a test asserts it appears above the first heading, because the useful version of that information is the one visible while deciding whether to start.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. The two gaps pull in opposite directions. Closing the prerequisite gaps lengthens paths past the point anyone finishes them; closing the coverage gap, four core concepts currently in no path, means more or wider paths, which worsens the prerequisite gaps. I do not think there is a clean answer. What I would not defend is where this started, with both gaps unmeasured and a promise on every page that the data contradicted.

    One sharper point. Sixty-two of the 106 concepts sit in exactly one path. A path that is the sole route to a third of a corpus is doing navigation work rather than curriculum work, and calling it curriculum obscures that the real need was findability.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. Paths held as data, pages generated from them, an ordering check and a coverage report in CI, and an entry state computed per path.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Take your longest learning path and count how many steps depend on something it never teaches. If that number is not zero, the page's promise about ordering is false, and the fix is one computed paragraph.
    2. Count the concepts your paths do not reach, split by whether you consider them essential. The essential ones in no path are your real finding.
    3. Count how many paths each item appears in. Anything appearing once is navigation, not curriculum, and should probably be solved by search instead.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. This guide holds nine paths over 106 concepts in concepts.yaml, generates every path page from them, and fails CI on an ordering violation; the entry-state paragraph and its test were added by the exercise above. The honest limits: no reader has completed any of these paths, so there is no evidence the orders are good, only that they are consistent with the prerequisite graph, and the demonstrated page records that distinction.
