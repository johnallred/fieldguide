    Search and query log analysis¶

    Query logs are the only direct statement of what readers wanted, and an attempt to substitute a hand-built retrieval proxy for them measures the proxy.

    supporting · difficulty 2 · 3h

    Adjacent, not quoted

    Not named in either posting. The input that turns a hand-written evaluation set into a representative one.

    Prerequisites: Findability and search design, Documentation analytics

    What it is¶

    Reading what people typed into your search box. It is the highest-value reader-side signal in documentation and among the cheapest to collect, and it differs in kind from everything else on a dashboard: page views say what people found, queries say what they were looking for, including every time they did not find it.

    The most useful single output is the zero-result list, which is a list, in readers' own words, of content you do not have.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because every other way of deciding what readers want is the author guessing.

    A documentation roadmap is usually assembled from support escalations, the loudest internal stakeholder, and what the team believes is missing. Queries are the only input coming from readers who were not motivated enough to file a ticket, which is almost all of them.

    The reason this sits beside retrieval evaluation in this curriculum is narrower and more interesting. A golden set of questions is hand-written, so it encodes the author's model of what readers ask. Query logs are what turn that set from plausible into representative, and without them there is no way to know which it is.

    The golden path¶

    Try to do this without logs, fail, and work out what the failure is worth. About thirty minutes. The first measurement gets published nowhere.

    Step 1: attempt the substitute

    With no query logs, the obvious move is to simulate the reader. Take the most basic query anyone would type, a page's own name, and check whether that page comes back first. Score it over the site's real search index of 1,358 entries:

    def search(q, limit=5):
    qt = toks(q)
    for loc, t in docs:
        tf = Counter(t)
        s = sum((tf[w] / len(t)) * math.log(N / (1 + df[w])) for w in qt if w in tf)

    concepts searched by their own title: 90
rank of their own page: {1: 7, 2: 14, 3: 10, '4+': 42, 'none': 17}

not first for their own name: 83
   Information architecture   rank 9     top: paths/content-architect/#1-information-architecture
   Diátaxis                   rank None  top: paths/developer-educator/#5-designing-tutorials
   Content models             rank None  top: concepts/content-data-transformation/#read-first

    Seven of ninety pages rank first for their own title. A catastrophic findability result with an obvious story attached: generated navigation pages carry concept names as headings, they are short, and a short document with the term in its heading beats the long page that explains it.

    The story is wrong.

    Step 2: check the mechanism before believing the number

    That story claims generated pages duplicate concept titles and compete with them. The claim is structural, so it can be checked with no scorer at all:

    concept titles appearing as an index entry's title outside their own page: 1 distinct
total such competing entries: 1
   Time to first success   concepts/quickstarts/#time-to-first-success   77 words

    One. There is no duplication problem. The 7-of-90 figure came from my own length normalisation: dividing term frequency by document length rewards short sections, and a path page's one-line entry for a concept is about as short as a document gets. MkDocs Material uses lunr, with boosted title fields and different length handling, and does not rank this way.

    I had built a proxy for search in order to avoid needing query logs, and the proxy measured itself. That is this page's lesson, reached the expensive way: a hand-built retrieval model is not a substitute for knowing what readers typed, because its failures are indistinguishable from findings.

    Step 3: measure what does not depend on a ranking function

    Three properties are structural, so no scorer can corrupt them:

    complete pages whose own title words are absent from their text: 0/106
pages absent from the search index:                                 0
index entry titles that are not unique:                            42

    The first two are clean. The third is the real finding:

entries carrying one of those seven titles: 751 of 1505  (50%)

    Half the search index carries one of seven titles. The page contract that makes this corpus consistent, and that CI enforces, has produced 751 sections indistinguishable by title.

    The disambiguating information exists: every entry carries its location, so the page is recoverable, and Material's search UI uses it and shows the parent page, so a human browsing the site is fine. Any consumer using title alone is not, and that now includes retrieval pipelines and assistants reading an index directly. It is the same defect as a grounding report naming the page when it checked the chunk: the identifier has to carry the section, or ninety-one things look like one thing.

    Step 4: the test logs would have passed

    A golden set is meant to be representative. Twenty cases, hand-written by the author of the corpus. Then a real reader arrived with a real question, the only genuine query this project has ever received:

    what is docpack actually creating or doing, and what should I be looking at

    Scored against the twenty:

    closest golden case: llms-txt-what   what is llms.txt and who proposed it      shared key terms: 1
closest golden case: install         how do I install docpack                  shared key terms: 1
closest golden case: exit-codes      what exit codes does the command return   shared key terms: 1

    One shared key term, at best. A twenty-case set written specifically to represent reader questions held nothing resembling the first real one, and the missing case was not exotic: it was "what does this thing do", which is the question every reader has first.

    One query is not a sample. The direction is not ambiguous either. The author wrote twenty questions about what the corpus explains, and the reader asked about what it had not thought to say.

    Step 5: so collect them from day one

    Internal search logging is a few lines of configuration and the only instrumentation in this field that reports content you do not have. Everything else measures the content you do.

    What you built. A retrieval proxy that produced a plausible wrong finding, the structural check that disproved it, three measurements that survive without a scorer including 47 per cent of an index sharing seven titles, and a demonstration that a hand-written query set missed the first real question it met.

    Reference¶

    What queries say that nothing else does¶

    Signal Means
    Zero-result queries Content you do not have, in readers' words
    High volume, low click-through The page exists and the result looks wrong
    Queries that are error messages Readers arrive with a symptom, not a topic
    Words your docs never use A vocabulary gap, not a content gap
    Queries repeated within a session The first result failed
    A query straight after a page view That page did not answer

    The fourth row is the most commonly misread. A term you do not use is usually not a missing page, it is a missing synonym on a page you already have, and writing a new page is the expensive way to fix it.

    What to do with each finding¶

    Finding Response
    Zero results, concept missing Write it
    Zero results, concept present Add the reader's words to the page
    High volume, wrong page first Fix titles, summaries and headings
    Error-message queries Index error strings; readers paste them verbatim
    A query refined twice in a session Watch the session, not the query
    Queries for things you removed A retirement redirect is missing

    Without logs, what is still honest¶

    Check Needs a ranking function
    Does each page contain its own key terms No
    Is every page in the index No
    Are index entry titles unique No
    Are meta descriptions distinct No
    Do synonyms for each concept appear anywhere No
    Does each page rank first for its own name Yes, see step 2
    Which of two retrievers is better Yes

    Everything above the line is worth doing on day zero. Everything below needs either the real search engine or real queries, and a scorer you wrote yourself will answer confidently and wrongly.

    Privacy, briefly¶

    Query strings are user-submitted text, and people paste surprising things into search boxes: internal hostnames, tokens, customer names. Aggregate before storing, keep a short retention window, and do not log queries alongside an identity unless someone agreed to that. None of this is a reason to skip collection; it is a reason to collect the aggregate rather than the transcript.

    Why this concept is adjacent rather than quoted¶

    Neither posting names search logs, and both ask for measurement that depends on them. NVIDIA's "AI answer quality" and "repeat questions" are each evaluation over a question set, and a question set without query data is the author's guess about readers, which is exactly the gap step 4 measures.

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with the asymmetry. "Page views tell me what people found. Queries tell me what they were looking for, including every time they did not find it. The zero-result list is the only artifact in documentation analytics that describes content you do not have, and it costs a few lines of configuration to start collecting."

    The follow-up is what I did without logs, and the honest answer is that I got it wrong. I built a scorer over my own search index to check whether each page ranks first for its own name, and got seven of ninety, which looked like a catastrophe with a tidy explanation about generated pages competing for terms. The explanation was structural, so I checked it, and exactly one competing entry existed. The seven-of-ninety was my own length normalisation rewarding short sections. I had built a proxy for search to avoid needing query data, and measured the proxy.

    What survives without logs is worth saying too. Structural checks cannot be corrupted by a scorer: every page contains its own key terms, every page is in the index, and 751 of 1,505 entries carry one of seven titles because the page contract gives every page the same seven sections. A human browsing Material is fine, since the UI shows the parent page, but anything consuming title alone, a retrieval pipeline included, sees ninety-one identical things.

    The point to close on. My golden set has twenty hand-written questions and was meant to be representative. The first real reader asked what the thing actually produces and what he should be looking at, and that shared at most one key term with any of the twenty. One query is not a sample, but the direction is not ambiguous: I wrote twenty questions about what the corpus explains, and the reader asked about what it had not thought to say. Logs close that gap and nothing else does.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. Search logging enabled on a corpus you own, and a weekly zero-result list somebody actually reads.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Turn on internal search logging if it is off. It is the cheapest instrumentation in this field and the only one that reports absence.
    2. Compare your evaluation question set against the last ten real questions you received from anybody, counting shared terms. Predict the number first; mine was one.
    3. Check whether your index entry titles are unique. If your pages share a template they are not, and any consumer reading titles without locations is presenting duplicates.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. The structural measurements run against this guide's built search index and are reproducible; the failed proxy and its disproof are both recorded above rather than tidied away. What does not exist is a single query log: no site has been published, so there are no queries, no zero-result list and no click-through data, and the one real reader question in step 4 arrived in conversation rather than through a search box. The demonstrated page records the concept as having no demonstration on the log side.
