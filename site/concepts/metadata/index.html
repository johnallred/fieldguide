    Metadata for navigation and retrieval¶

    Two audiences read your front matter, they want different fields, and a field with no reader is already wrong.

    core · difficulty 2 · 3h

    Asked for by name

    NVIDIA: "content modeling, taxonomy, and metadata for both navigation and machine retrieval"

    Prerequisites: Content models

    Leads to: Findability and search design, Agent-ready content

    What it is¶

    The structured data attached to each page, usually in front matter. NVIDIA's phrasing is unusually precise and worth taking literally: metadata for both navigation and machine retrieval. Those are two audiences with different needs, and a single field set rarely serves both well.

    Navigation metadata answers "where does this page belong and what do I call it in a sidebar". Retrieval metadata answers "which subset of the corpus should a query search". They overlap and they are not the same, and the mistake is designing one and assuming it covers the other.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because the retrieval half is new and most corpora do not have it.

    Navigation metadata is well understood: a title, a position, a section. It has existed as long as documentation sites have. Retrieval metadata is what makes a filtered search possible, what lets a chunk carry its context into a vector store, and what an assistant uses to decide where to look. NVIDIA asking for both in one phrase is a signal that they know the second is a separate design problem.

    The uncomfortable part, and the subject of the golden path, is that adding fields is easy and keeping them true is not. A field written by a generator and read by nothing will be wrong within weeks, and nothing will report it.

    The golden path¶

    Audit a real corpus's metadata, find the field that is lying, and work out why nobody noticed. About thirty minutes.

    Step 1: measure coverage, not existence

    121 pages

field        pages  coverage
title          121      100%
summary        121      100%
diataxis       121      100%
audience       121      100%
concept         64       52%
tags           64       52%
generated      53       43%

    Coverage is the first question because an optional field cannot be built on. A filter on tags here would silently exclude 48 per cent of the corpus, and the result would look like a retrieval problem rather than a metadata problem.

    Step 2: check that the populated fields are true

    Full coverage is necessary and not sufficient. Split each field's values by whether the page was generated:

    generated pages by diataxis: {'reference': 53}
hand-written by diataxis:    {'how-to': 22, 'explanation': 46}

    diataxis had 100 per cent coverage, three legal values, and was wrong on 53 pages. The placeholder generator hardcoded reference, so that value meant "not written yet" while no real page used it.

    A filter for reference documentation would have returned exclusively stubs.

    Step 3: count the readers

    grep -rn "diataxis" src/fieldguide/*.py hooks/*.py mkdocs.yml

    src/fieldguide/generate.py:144:        "diataxis": "reference",
src/fieldguide/scaffold.py:102:        "diataxis: explanation",

    Two writers, zero readers. That is the explanation for step 2: nothing consumed the field, so nothing could notice it was wrong.

    The reader count is the diagnostic. Write it down for every field you have. A field with more writers than readers is decoration, and decoration rots.

    Step 4: check the field is even parseable

    m = re.match(r"^---\n(.*?)\n---\n", p.read_text(), re.S)
try:
    yaml.safe_load(m.group(1))
except yaml.YAMLError:
    bad.append(p)

    front matter parses: 75 pages
front matter INVALID: 46 pages

    Forty-six pages whose front matter no YAML parser could read, because a generated summary contained an unquoted colon. MkDocs' own metadata parser is more forgiving than YAML, so the build was green and the pages looked fine.

    The visible symptom was in the wrong place entirely:

    meta description: A validated curriculum for documentation engineering, ...

    Every one of those 46 pages fell back to the site description, because its own summary was unreadable. A navigation-and-SEO symptom caused by a serialisation bug, which is why this check belongs next to the metadata work rather than in the SEO work.

    Step 5: fix all three classes

    Problem Fix
    Unparseable front matter Serialise with yaml.safe_dump, never f-strings
    A field that lies Add the honest value: diataxis: placeholder
    A field with no reader Give it one, so drift fails a build 
    ok: every page's front matter parses as YAML
all pages by diataxis: {'how-to': 22, 'explanation': 46, 'placeholder': 53}
  FIRES: diataxis is 'refrence', which is not one of [...]

    What you learned. Metadata health has three questions and most audits ask only the first: is it present, is it true, and does anything read it.

    Reference¶

    The two audiences, and what each wants¶

    Field Navigation Retrieval
    title Essential, it is the label Useful, it is high-signal text
    summary Sidebar hints, meta description Essential, it is what a map lists
    diataxis or page type Section grouping Essential filter: "how-to only"
    audience Sometimes a separate route Essential filter
    position or order Essential Useless
    product_version A version switcher Essential filter
    component or area Grouping Essential filter
    updated or reviewed A date in the footer, with caveats Staleness filter
    tags Weak; a tag index few people use Weak unless closed and required
    canonical Not used Deduplication across versions

    The asymmetry in the position row is the clearest illustration of the two audiences. Navigation needs ordering and retrieval does not care. Retrieval needs filters and navigation can live without most of them.

    What a chunk needs that a page does not¶

    When a page is chunked for retrieval, each chunk leaves home without its context. The metadata a chunk has to carry:

    Field Why the chunk needs it
    Source page and anchor So an answer can cite a link a reader can follow
    Page title A chunk from the middle of a page has no title of its own
    Heading trail Which section this is, so the chunk is placeable
    Page type and audience So a filtered query still works at chunk level
    Version So a 2.3 chunk is not returned to a 2.4 reader

    Page-level metadata that is not copied onto chunks is metadata that stops existing the moment retrieval starts, which is the most common reason filtered search works in a prototype and not in production.

    How many fields¶

    Count Outcome
    Three or four, required, all consumed Reliable, and the common good answer
    Eight or more, half optional Half-populated, nothing depends on it
    Any number with no consumers Decorative, and already wrong

    Start from the filter you want and add the field that makes it possible. Fields added because they seem like good practice are the ones that end up lying.

    Where metadata lives¶

    Location Good for Watch out
    Front matter Per-page facts authors control Must be valid YAML; see step 4
    A registry or data file Relationships, ordering, cross-page rules Must not disagree with the pages
    Derived at build time Word count, last modified, heading trail Cheap, and invisible to source consumers
    The file path Section, product area Encoded in a URL, so expensive to change

    Deriving is underrated. A heading trail, a word count or a reading time computed at build time cannot drift, because nothing stores it.

    How to talk about it¶

    Take NVIDIA's phrasing literally. "Navigation metadata and retrieval metadata are different field sets. Navigation needs ordering and a label; retrieval needs filters, and it needs them copied onto every chunk, because a chunk leaves the page without its context. A corpus with good navigation metadata and no retrieval metadata looks fine until you put a search in front of it."

    The follow-up is how you keep it honest, and the answer is reader count. A field with more writers than readers will be wrong and nothing will report it. I had a fully populated, closed-vocabulary field that was wrong on 44 per cent of pages for exactly that reason.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Every required field is a tax on contribution and a thing reviewers have to check, so the right number is small. The pressure runs the other way, because each new filter suggests a new field, and the way to resist it is to require a named consumer before adding one.

    One sharper point. Check that your front matter is valid YAML. MkDocs' metadata parser is more forgiving than YAML, so an unquoted colon builds cleanly and is invisible to every tool using a real parser. I shipped 46 such pages, and the only visible symptom was their meta descriptions falling back to the site default. Four lines of check, and it is the cheapest AI-readiness work available.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A metadata audit of a corpus you own: coverage per field, value distribution per field, and reader count per field.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Print coverage and value distribution for every field in your corpus. Heavy skew toward one value usually means a default is being written somewhere.
    2. Count readers for each field. Anything with none is either removable or needs a consumer.
    3. Parse all your front matter with a real YAML parser. This takes four lines and finds something more often than it should.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. docpack carries metadata through into its retrieval artifacts, so each chunk keeps its source, heading trail and page type, and summary is indexed for search after an evaluation showed it was being ignored. The more useful story is the audit above: a field at 100 per cent coverage, legal values, zero readers, wrong on 44 per cent of pages, and the serialisation bug underneath it.
