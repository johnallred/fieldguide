    Content models¶

    A schema for your content, judged by the specific lies it makes impossible.

    core · difficulty 2 · 4h

    Asked for by name

    NVIDIA: "Information architecture skills: content modeling, taxonomy, and metadata for both navigation and machine retrieval"

    Prerequisites: Information architecture

    Leads to: Taxonomy and controlled vocabularies, Metadata for navigation and retrieval, Content lifecycle and governance, Single-sourcing and content reuse

    What it is¶

    A schema for your content: what kinds of page exist, what fields each kind carries, which of those fields are required, and which draw from a closed vocabulary. In a docs-as-code project it usually lives in front matter plus a validator.

    The way to judge one is not by how elegant the schema is. It is by asking what specific lies it makes impossible, and then checking that each of those lies actually fails a build. The golden path does exactly that, twelve times.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    NVIDIA lists content modeling first in its information architecture requirement, ahead of taxonomy and metadata, which is the right order: the model is the thing the other two attach to.

    The reason it is engineering rather than documentation theory is that a model without a validator is a convention, and conventions decay silently. Every corpus has a style guide saying how pages should be structured. Very few can tell you which pages currently violate it.

    It is also the foundation of everything in the AI-ready category. Filtering retrieval by page type, chunking by section, assembling an llms.txt by audience: all of those read fields. If the fields are optional, inconsistent or unparseable, none of it works, and the failure looks like a bad retriever rather than a bad schema.

    The golden path¶

    Take a real content model and attack it. Twelve defects, one at a time, and check what survives. About forty minutes.

    Step 1: the model under test

    This guide's own registry is a content model with a validator. Its closed vocabularies and its page contract:

    TIERS      = ("core", "supporting", "context")
PROVENANCE = ("quoted", "derived", "adjacent")
STATUSES   = ("planned", "drafted", "complete")

REQUIRED_SECTIONS = (
    "What it is", "Why the job asks for it", "The golden path",
    "Reference", "How to talk about it", "How to prove it",
)
MIN_COMPLETE_WORDS = 450

    Plus structural rules: every concept needs at least one demanded_by entry, prerequisites must resolve to real concepts, and the prerequisite graph must be acyclic.

    Step 2: attack the vocabularies and the graph

    Mutate a copy of the registry, once per defect, and record where each is caught:

    def attempt(label, mutate):
    data = copy.deepcopy(base)
    mutate(data)
    Path("mutated.yaml").write_text(yaml.safe_dump(data, sort_keys=False))
    try:
        registry = load_registry(Path("mutated.yaml"))
    except RegistryError as e:
        print(f"  CAUGHT at parse  {label}")
        return
    findings = validate_against_tree(registry, Path("docs"))
    print(f"  {'CAUGHT at check' if not findings.ok else 'MISSED'}  {label}")

      CAUGHT at parse  tier outside the vocabulary
  CAUGHT at parse  status outside the vocabulary
  CAUGHT at parse  provenance outside the vocabulary
  CAUGHT at parse  prerequisite that does not exist
  CAUGHT at parse  prerequisite cycle
  CAUGHT at parse  duplicate concept id
  CAUGHT at parse  concept with no demanded_by

    Seven for seven, all at parse time, with messages that name the offender:

    concepts[5] (metadata): tier must be one of ['core', 'supporting', 'context']
prerequisite cycle: content-model -> metadata -> content-model
duplicate concept id 'metadata'

    Step 3: attack the page contract

    The remaining defects are about the relationship between the registry and the files, so they are caught later, by a validator that reads both:

      CAUGHT check  complete concept, page deleted
  CAUGHT check  complete page truncated below 450 words
  CAUGHT check  complete page missing a required section
  CAUGHT check  an orphan page with no concept

    Four for four. Note the last one: a page nobody registered is as much a model violation as a registry entry with no page. A model that only validates in one direction lets the corpus grow pages that no index knows about.

    Step 4: find the defect the model did not cover

    Twelve caught is a good result, and it is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what was missing from the list.

    Parse the front matter of every page with a real YAML parser:

    for p in sorted(Path("docs").rglob("*.md")):
    m = re.match(r"^---\n(.*?)\n---\n", p.read_text(), re.S)
    if not m:
        continue
    try:
        yaml.safe_load(m.group(1))
    except yaml.YAMLError as e:
        bad.append(p)

    front matter parses: 75 pages
front matter INVALID: 46 pages

    Forty-six of 121 pages had front matter that no YAML parser could read. The placeholder generator assembled it with f-strings, so the generated summary read summary: Outline only: Measuring AI answer quality is ... and the unquoted colon started a mapping.

    Every check above passed on that corpus. The build passed too, because MkDocs' own metadata parser is more forgiving than YAML. The visible consequence was silent:

    meta description: A validated curriculum for documentation engineering, ...

    The page's own summary was unreadable, so the description fell back to the site default on all 46 pages.

    Step 5: fix the cause, not the pages

    def _front_matter(title, summary, audience="new-user"):
    data = {"title": title, "summary": summary, "diataxis": "reference",
            "audience": audience, "generated": True}
    body = yaml.safe_dump(data, sort_keys=False, default_flow_style=False)
    return f"---\n{body}---\n\n"

    front matter parses: 118   invalid: 3

    The remaining three were hand-written pages with the same mistake, including this one's own summary. Quote them, and add the check so the class cannot return:

    ok: every page's front matter parses as YAML

    What you learned. A content model is judged by the lies it prevents, and the useful question about any model is which lie is still possible. This one prevented twelve and was blind to the thirteenth, which was the one that mattered most for machine readability.

    Reference¶

    The parts of a content model¶

    Part Example here Enforced by
    Page types diataxis: tutorial / how-to / reference / explanation Closed vocabulary
    Required fields title, summary, diataxis, audience Parse-time check
    Closed vocabularies tier, status, provenance Membership test
    Relationships prerequisites, acyclic Graph check
    Structural contract Six required sections, 450 words File check
    Both-directions coverage No page without a concept, no concept without a page File check
    Serialisation validity Front matter parses as YAML The check added in step 5

    The last row is the one most models omit, and it is the cheapest of all of them.

    Where each class of defect is caught¶

    Caught at Defects
    Parse Vocabulary violations, dangling references, cycles, duplicates, missing required fields
    Validation against files Status disagreeing with reality, contract violations, orphan pages
    Build Nothing structural; the build is not your content model
    Never, without the step 5 check Front matter that is not valid YAML

    How to write the model down¶

    Option Good for Cost
    A data file plus a validator A corpus with structure worth enforcing You write the validator
    JSON Schema on front matter Standard tooling, editor integration Awkward for cross-page rules
    A linter with custom rules Teams already running Vale Rules are per-field, not relational
    A documented convention Nothing; it decays Zero to write, high to maintain

    Cross-page rules are the deciding factor. A schema validates one page at a time, so prerequisite resolution, cycle detection and orphan detection need code. That is why this guide's model is a Python module rather than a JSON Schema.

    Designing one for a real corpus¶

    Question Why it matters
    What page types exist, really? Count what you have before inventing categories
    Which fields would change a reader's path? Those belong in navigation
    Which fields would filter a retrieval query? Those belong in metadata
    What is required versus optional? An optional field is absent half the time, so nothing can depend on it
    What vocabulary is closed? Open vocabularies drift; see taxonomy
    What relationships exist between pages? These need code, not a schema

    The fourth row is the one with teeth. A field that is optional is a field your pipeline cannot rely on, so "optional metadata" is usually a decision not to use it for anything.

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with lies, not schemas. "I judge a content model by the specific lies it makes impossible. On this guide's own registry I could not mark a concept complete without a page, could not add a prerequisite that does not exist, could not create a cycle, and could not ship a complete page under 450 words or missing a required section. Twelve defect classes, each one a failing build."

    The follow-up is about contributor friction, and the honest answer is that every required field is a tax and the right number is small. Four required fields that everything depends on beats twelve that half the corpus omits, because an optional field cannot be built on.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Cross-page rules need code, which means your content model becomes software you maintain, with its own tests and its own bugs. My own validator had a counting bug that double-reported demonstrations and a generator that emitted invalid YAML for 38% of the corpus. A model with a validator is better than a convention, and it is not free.

    One sharper point. Front matter is the machine-readable half of a page, and almost nobody checks that it parses. MkDocs' metadata parser is more forgiving than YAML, so a page with an unquoted colon builds cleanly and is invisible to every tool that uses a real parser. I shipped 46 such pages. That check is four lines and it is the cheapest AI-readiness work available, because a corpus that cannot be parsed cannot be filtered, chunked or retrieved.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A content model with a validator, plus the list of defects it catches, each with a test.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run step 4 against a corpus you own. Parse every page's front matter with a real YAML parser and count the failures. This finds something surprisingly often.
    2. Write down the lies your current model prevents. If the list is short or you cannot name one, you have a convention rather than a model.
    3. Pick the most consequential lie that is still possible and make it fail. One check, with a test that proves it fires.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. docpack validates five required front-matter fields, two of them closed vocabularies, rejected by docpack docs --check. This guide's registry adds the relational rules a per-page schema cannot express. The part worth discussing in an interview is step 4: the model that caught twelve things and missed the thirteenth, and how the thirteenth was found.
