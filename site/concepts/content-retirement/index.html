    Retiring content¶

    Deleting a page without breaking the links to it, and why corpora only ever grow.

    supporting · difficulty 2 · 3h

    Asked for by name

    NVIDIA: "Measure outcomes such as time-to-first-success, support deflection, repeat questions, and AI answer quality, and use the data to decide what to build, fix, or retire"

    Prerequisites: Content lifecycle and governance

    What it is¶

    Removing a page from the corpus on purpose: deciding it should go, redirecting its URL, and dealing with everything that pointed at it. NVIDIA lists retire alongside build and fix as one of three outcomes of measurement, which is more than most teams do, because most teams have no third option.

    The mechanics are easy. The reason corpora only ever grow is not technical.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because an unretired corpus degrades in a way that no amount of writing fixes. Every stale page competes with a current one in search, dilutes retrieval, and costs a reviewer attention. Past a certain size the most valuable documentation work available is deletion, and nobody is authorised to do it.

    NVIDIA naming retirement as a measurement outcome is the important part. The decision to retire needs evidence, which means it needs the measurement category to exist first, and it means the answer to "can we delete this" stops being a matter of opinion.

    The golden path¶

    Retire a page properly, then find the configuration that decides whether a mistake is loud or silent. About twenty-five minutes.

    Step 1: a redirect

    mkdir -p retire-demo/docs && cd retire-demo
pip install --quiet mkdocs mkdocs-redirects

cat > mkdocs.yml <<'EOF'
site_name: R
plugins:
  - redirects:
      redirect_maps:
        old-guide.md: new-guide.md
EOF
printf '# New guide\n\nThe current content.\n' > docs/new-guide.md
printf '# Home\n\nSee the [new guide](new-guide.md).\n' > docs/index.md

python3 -m mkdocs build -q

    Step 2: see what the retired URL now serves

    python3 -c "
import re
from pathlib import Path
h = Path('site/old-guide/index.html').read_text()
print(re.search(r'<meta[^>]*refresh[^>]*>', h).group(0))
"

    <meta http-equiv="refresh" content="0; url=../new-guide/">

    A generated page containing a client-side meta refresh. Note what that is not: it is not an HTTP 301. The distinction matters, because a 301 transfers accumulated ranking signals to the new URL and a meta refresh is a weaker, slower signal that search engines treat with more suspicion. On static hosting this is often all you can do; if you control the server or the CDN, prefer a real 301 and keep the meta refresh as the fallback.

    Step 3: point the redirect at nothing

    sed -i 's/new-guide.md$/gone.md/' mkdocs.yml
python3 -m mkdocs build 2>&1 | grep -i redirect
echo "exit=$?"

    WARNING - Redirect target 'gone.md' does not exist!
exit=0

    The plugin notices, declines to generate the redirect page, and the build succeeds. So the retired URL becomes a hard 404 rather than a redirect to a 404, which is better than it could have been, and the build still exits zero.

    Step 4: make it loud

    python3 -m mkdocs build --strict >/dev/null 2>&1; echo "exit=$?"

    exit=1

    With --strict, a dangling redirect target fails the build. Measured behaviour, all three cases:

    Case Build Result
    Valid redirect exit 0 Meta refresh page generated
    Dangling target exit 0, with a warning No page; retired URL 404s
    Dangling target, --strict exit 1 Nothing ships

    Step 5: deal with what pointed at the page

    The redirect handles external links. Internal references are a separate problem and the one that is usually forgotten:

    grep -rn "old-guide" docs/ | grep -v "^docs/old-guide"

    Internal links should be rewritten to the new target, not left to bounce through the redirect. A corpus whose internal links all pass through redirects accumulates chains, and a chain eventually contains a broken hop that link checking cannot see, because each individual hop resolves.

    What you built. A retirement that redirects, a check that makes a mistaken target fail, and a rule about internal links that keeps chains from forming.

    Reference¶

    The retirement checklist¶

    Step Why
    Decide, with evidence Traffic, support deflection, repeat questions
    Pick the successor, or accept there is none A redirect needs a target
    Rewrite internal links to the successor Prevents redirect chains
    Add the redirect External links and bookmarks
    Remove it from navigation and any index Otherwise it is reachable and gone
    Remove it from retrieval artifacts A deleted page still in a vector store still answers
    Note the retirement somewhere So the decision is not relitigated annually

    The retrieval row is new and easy to miss. A page removed from the site but still present in an index or an llms.txt keeps answering questions from beyond the grave, and the answer looks authoritative.

    When not to redirect¶

    Situation Do this instead
    No successor exists A 410 Gone, or a tombstone page explaining the removal
    The content was wrong, not outdated Say so on the tombstone; a silent redirect looks evasive
    The page documented a removed feature Point at the migration path, not the nearest page
    It is one of many pages merged into one Redirect to the anchor, not just the page

    Redirecting to a vaguely related page is worse than a 404. A reader who lands somewhere that does not answer their question and shows no sign of knowing what they asked has had their time wasted twice.

    Why corpora only grow¶

    Obstacle Reality
    Nobody owns deletion Authoring has owners; removal usually does not
    It feels like destroying work Git has it forever; this fear is unfounded and real
    Someone might need it Possibly, and the measurement tells you
    It might break a link Solved, and it is the stated reason more often than the real one
    No policy exists So every deletion is a negotiation

    The fix is a written policy that makes deletion the default outcome for pages meeting stated criteria, so that removal needs no new argument each time. That is governance, not tooling, which is why this page sits downstream of content lifecycle.

    Evidence that justifies retirement¶

    Signal Threshold worth discussing
    Page views Near zero over a full release cycle
    Search exits People find it and immediately leave
    Support tickets citing it Readers are being misled by it
    Its subject no longer exists Automatic candidate
    Its freshness queue age Perpetually overdue and never claimed

    The last row is the useful one in practice. A page that has been in the review queue for four quarters without an owner claiming it is a page nobody is willing to maintain, and that is the evidence.

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with why it does not happen. "The mechanics of retirement are a redirect and a link rewrite. The reason corpora only grow is that authoring has owners and deletion does not, so every removal is a fresh negotiation. The fix is a written policy that makes deletion the default for pages meeting stated criteria."

    The follow-up is usually about breaking links, and it is worth naming as the stated reason more often than the real one. Redirects solve the external case; internal links get rewritten rather than bounced, because chains accumulate a broken hop that link checking cannot see.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Redirects are not free: each one is a permanent entry in a map that grows forever, and after a few years the redirect map is its own maintenance problem with chains and loops in it. Periodically collapsing chains and dropping redirects for URLs that have had no traffic for years is real work that nobody schedules.

    One sharper point. A retired page still in a retrieval index keeps answering questions. The site has deleted it, the vector store has not, and the answer arrives with a citation to a URL that now 404s. Retirement has to reach the retrieval artifacts, and that step is missing from essentially every retirement checklist written before 2023.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A retirement that happened: a redirect, the internal links rewritten in the same commit, and a written policy that authorised it.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run the golden path. Step 3 and step 4 together are the point: the mistake is silent unless you have --strict.
    2. Find your lowest-traffic page and try to retire it. Whatever blocks you is the actual obstacle, and it is probably not technical.
    3. Check whether anything in your retrieval pipeline would notice a deleted page. Usually nothing does.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Neither of this guide's repositories has retired anything, which the demonstrated page records, and a project with no readers has no retirement evidence to act on. What travels is the measured behaviour of the mechanism and the retrieval-index point, which is the part of the checklist most teams are currently missing.

    How this would be demonstrated elsewhere¶

    A project with no readers has nothing to retire, so the mechanism here is measured and the decision is not. Retirement is mostly a political act and the evidence is that somebody agreed to lose something.

    What you need. A corpus old enough to contain pages nobody should read, and the authority to remove them. The second is the scarce one.

    What a learner would do. Find the five oldest pages in a corpus you own, check whether each still describes a thing that exists, and then try to delete one. Record what happened: who objected, on what grounds, and what the compromise was.

    What you would see.

    Outcome What it means
    Deleted with a redirect The healthy case, and the rarest
    Marked deprecated instead The usual compromise; now you maintain it forever
    Kept because someone links to it The link is the argument; check whether anyone follows it
    Kept because removing it feels risky The real reason, and it is not a documentation reason
    Nobody could say who owns it The finding worth reporting

    The last row is what the exercise usually produces, and it is more useful than the deletion: a page nobody owns is a page nobody will update either.

    Why it does not commit. A deletion leaves no artifact except an absence and a redirect. The judgment, that this page cost more than it was worth, is a conversation, and the strongest available evidence is a redirect map with a reason beside each entry.
