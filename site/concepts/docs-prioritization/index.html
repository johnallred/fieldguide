    Prioritizing documentation work¶

    Deciding what to build, fix and retire from evidence rather than from whoever asked most recently.

    core · difficulty 2 · 3h

    use the data to decide what to build, fix, or retire

    Source: nvidia posting, verbatim

    Prerequisites: Repeat-question analysis, A systems mindset

    What it is¶

    Choosing which documentation work to do from evidence rather than from whoever asked most recently. The output is a ranked list with a stated basis, and crucially a retire column, which most documentation backlogs do not have.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    NVIDIA's phrasing is one clause at the end of the measurement bullet: "Measure outcomes such as time-to-first-success, support deflection, repeat questions, and AI answer quality, and use the data to decide what to build, fix, or retire."

    The three verbs are the content. Most documentation prioritisation has two options, build and fix, and a backlog that therefore only grows. Retire is the one that makes the other two sustainable, and it is in the posting explicitly.

    On a Tuesday this is the thing that decides whether you are useful. A documentation backlog at a company this size has a few hundred items: requests from engineers, gaps you found, support escalations, a migration somebody promised, and forty pages marked for review. You can do maybe three things this week. Without a basis, the three you do are whichever came with the most senior request attached, which is a reasonable heuristic and an expensive one.

    The reason it is core: the backlog in this field is unbounded. The product ships continuously, every release creates potential gaps, and the corpus ages whether you touch it or not. Prioritisation is not an occasional planning activity, it is the mechanism that converts an infinite queue into a finite week.

    The golden path¶

    Build a ranking from evidence you can actually get, and see it disagree with your instinct. Thirty minutes.

    Step 1: get the inputs that are cheap and the ones that are not

    mkdir -p prio-drill && cd prio-drill
cat > inputs.md <<'EOF'
| Input                        | Cost to get        | What it tells you |
|------------------------------|--------------------|-------------------|
| Support ticket topics        | cheap (export)     | what fails in the field |
| Repeat questions in channels | cheap (search)     | what docs failed to answer |
| Page views                   | cheap (analytics)  | demand, not satisfaction |
| Search queries with no click | cheap, underused   | a gap, stated in the reader's words |
| Time to first success        | expensive (study)  | whether the golden path works |
| Staleness by review date     | free (front matter)| risk, not impact |
| Requests from engineers      | free (they arrive) | what a colleague noticed |

The fourth row is the best value in the list and almost nobody uses it.
A query with no click is a reader telling you, in their own words, that
the answer is not there.
EOF
cat inputs.md

    Step 2: score on impact and cost, and write down the basis

    cat > backlog.csv <<'EOF'
item,action,evidence,affected_per_month,effort_hours,basis
Batch size guidance,build,"12 support tickets, 3 repeat asks",40,4,tickets
Rewrite install page,fix,"search: 'install fails' 60/mo no click",300,6,search
Migrate 40 pages to new model,fix,"migration commitment",0,30,commitment
USD schema reference,build,"2 engineer requests",8,20,request
Retire 2.x upgrade guides,retire,"0 views in 6 months, 11 pages",0,2,analytics
Fix 7 stale pages flagged by CI,fix,"review date >365d",25,5,staleness
Tutorial for the agentic toolkit,build,"product launch in 3 weeks",200,16,launch
EOF
column -s, -t backlog.csv

    Step 3: rank it mechanically, then look at what moved

    python3 - <<'PY'
import csv

rows = list(csv.DictReader(open("backlog.csv")))
for row in rows:
    affected = float(row["affected_per_month"])
    effort = float(row["effort_hours"])
    # Deliberately crude: people helped per hour of work. The number is not
    # the decision, it is a way to notice when your instinct disagrees with
    # the evidence and to make yourself say why.
    row["score"] = affected / effort if effort else 0.0

rows.sort(key=lambda r: -r["score"])
print(f"{'item':<34} {'action':<8} {'per mo':>7} {'hrs':>5} {'score':>7}  basis")
print("-" * 86)
for row in rows:
    print(f"{row['item']:<34} {row['action']:<8} "
          f"{row['affected_per_month']:>7} {row['effort_hours']:>5} "
          f"{row['score']:>7.1f}  {row['basis']}")
PY

    Step 4: argue with the ranking, in writing

    The ranking is an input, not a decision. This step is the actual skill.

    cat > decisions.md <<'EOF'
# This week

DO  Rewrite install page            (50.0)  highest score, and it is the
                                            first thing every developer touches
DO  Retire 2.x upgrade guides       ( 0.0)  scored zero and I am doing it anyway:
                                            2 hours, removes 11 pages from every
                                            future migration, audit and review
                                            cycle. The score cannot see avoided
                                            future cost.
DO  Batch size guidance             (10.0)  12 tickets is a real number and the
                                            fix is a decision guide, not a page

DEFER  Agentic toolkit tutorial     (12.5)  scored well, but the 200/month is a
                                            forecast rather than a measurement.
                                            Revisit when the launch date is firm.
DEFER  Migrate 40 pages             ( 0.0)  a commitment with no measured impact.
                                            Renegotiate the deadline rather than
                                            spending 30 hours on it this month.
DEFER  USD schema reference         ( 0.4)  20 hours for 8 people. Ask the two
                                            requesters whether a 2-hour decision
                                            guide would do instead.

NOTE  Staleness items scored 5.0 and are genuinely ambiguous: a stale page is
      a risk, not a measured harm. Doing them when they are cheap.
EOF
cat decisions.md

    What you built. A ranking, and a written record of the three places you overrode it. The overrides are the deliverable. A score nobody argues with is a score nobody is using; the point of making it explicit is to make the disagreements explicit too, so that next quarter you can tell whether your instincts were better than the number.

    Note the retire item: it scored zero and was done anyway, because the scoring function cannot see avoided future cost. That is a known limitation written down rather than a flaw in the method.

    Reference¶

    The three actions¶

    Action When Common mistake
    Build A gap with measured demand Building for forecast demand
    Fix Existing content failing readers Fixing by staleness date rather than by harm
    Retire Content with no readers, or superseded Never doing it, so the corpus only grows

    Why retire is load-bearing¶

    Every page you keep costs something forever, and the costs are invisible individually:

    Cost of a page nobody reads Paid by
    It appears in search results and in retrieval Every reader who gets it instead of the right page
    It is included in every audit and migration You, repeatedly
    It must be reviewed to stay in compliance with the model The review cycle
    It dilutes llms.txt and the chunk corpus Every assistant answer
    It can be found and followed, and be wrong A reader, expensively

    The fourth row is new and is the one worth raising in an interview. In a corpus packaged for retrieval, a stale page is not merely unread, it is competing. That is a materially stronger argument for retirement than it was five years ago.

    What each input is good and bad for¶

    Input Good for Misleads about
    Page views Demand Satisfaction. A heavily viewed page may be heavily re-read because it is bad
    Support tickets Real failures Only covers people who asked. Most do not
    Repeat questions Documentation gaps Biased toward whoever is loudest in a channel
    Zero-click searches Gaps in the reader's words Volume is low, so weak on frequency
    Staleness Risk Harm. An old page about something stable is fine
    Engineer requests What a colleague noticed Impact. Usually reflects their week

    Honest limits of scoring¶

    Limit Consequence
    Affected-per-month is usually a guess The ranking is precise about imprecise inputs
    It cannot see avoided future cost Retirement and tooling always score badly
    It cannot see strategic commitments A launch has value a ticket count does not capture
    It optimises for the readers you have Not the readers a new capability might attract
    Effort estimates are wrong And wrong in the optimistic direction

    A scoring function that is treated as a decision is worse than no scoring function, because it launders judgement as arithmetic. Treated as a prompt for disagreement, it is useful.

    How to talk about it¶

    What a competent answer sounds like. Lead with an override and with retire. "I score the backlog on people affected per hour of work, and then I write down where I disagree with the score. Last quarter the highest-value thing I did scored zero: retiring eleven pages about a version nobody runs. Two hours, and it removed them from every future audit and from the retrieval corpus, where they were competing with the current pages."

    The follow-up is "how do you say no to a senior person", and it is the real question. The answer that works is to make the comparison explicit rather than the refusal. You do not say no; you show the two items and what each is based on, and ask which they want. "This is 20 hours for 8 people, and this is 6 hours for 300 based on search data. I can do the first if it matters more for a reason the data cannot see." That reframes it from your judgement against theirs to a choice they are making with information.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Evidence-based prioritisation systematically underweights new things. Every input you can cheaply measure is about the readers you already have and the content that already exists, so a strict reading of the data never builds documentation for a capability that has not shipped. That is exactly backwards for release readiness, where the whole point is being ready before demand exists. The resolution is to carve out capacity for launch work outside the scored backlog, rather than trying to score a forecast, because a forecast dressed as a measurement corrupts the whole exercise.

    One sharper point. The most valuable prioritisation move in this field is usually not choosing between backlog items at all, it is noticing that several items share a cause and that the cause is fixable once. Four tickets about four pages being out of date is not four fix items; it is one freshness monitoring item. That is the systems mindset as a prioritisation technique, and it is how a backlog starts shrinking instead of being triaged forever.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A ranked backlog with a basis column and a written record of the overrides. One page, and it is unusual enough to be memorable.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Export your zero-click search queries for any documentation site you can access. It takes ten minutes, almost nobody does it, and the list is readers telling you the gaps in their own words.
    2. Find something to retire. Pages with no views in six months, or documentation for a version out of support. Propose it with the costs from the table above, including the retrieval-competition argument.
    3. Score a real backlog and write down your three overrides. The overrides are the artifact. A ranking is arithmetic; knowing when to ignore it is the job.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Anyone can say they are data-driven. A backlog with a basis column, and a documented case where you did the thing that scored zero and said why, demonstrates that you use evidence without hiding behind it.

    How this would be demonstrated elsewhere¶

    Prioritisation needs a backlog with competing claims on it and someone who can be overruled. A repository with one author has neither, so what is here is the reasoning and not the decisions.

    What you need. A real backlog, a source of demand data, and at least one stakeholder who wants something different from what the data says.

    What a learner would do. Take your next ten documentation tasks and give each a basis column: the ticket count, the search volume, the failing check, or "someone asked". Then rank by basis and compare with the order you would have chosen anyway.

    What you would see.

    Observation Typical What it tells you
    Tasks with a measurable basis Fewer than half The honest starting point
    Tasks whose basis is "someone asked" The largest group Not illegitimate, but it should be labelled
    Agreement between the data order and your instinct Partial Where it disagrees is the interesting part
    Tasks you did anyway, against the data Some, and that is fine Say why, in the column

    The fourth row is the demonstration. Anyone can claim to be data-driven; the specific, unfakeable artifact is a backlog where one item scored zero, was done anyway, and carries the reason.

    Why it does not commit. A ranked backlog is a snapshot of an argument. The evidence of skill is in having held the argument and recorded the exception, and that is a document in someone else's planning tool.
