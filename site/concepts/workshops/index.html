    Workshops and hands-on labs¶

    A three-hour workshop covers about one step of a thirty-four-hour path, so the design question is which step genuinely needs a room.

    supporting · difficulty 2 · 4h

    Adjacent, not quoted

    Neither posting mentions workshops. A standard developer-relations deliverable, and the only setting where you can watch a reader fail.

    Prerequisites: Learning paths and curriculum, Reproducible environments

    What it is¶

    A fixed block of time in which a group works through material together, with someone present who can unblock them. The defining constraint is not the content, it is synchrony: everyone has to be at the same place at the same time, so the slowest person sets the pace and any environment failure strands one attendee while the room moves on without them.

    Everything that makes workshop design different from writing a tutorial follows from that constraint.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Two reasons, and only one of them is about teaching.

    The honest teaching reason is that a room is the only place you can watch people fail. Assessment over a corpus tells you whether your tasks are well formed; it cannot tell you where readers get stuck. Twenty people attempting your quickstart in front of you produces that information in forty minutes, and it is the highest-quality signal available anywhere in this job.

    The other reason is that workshops are a developer-relations deliverable with a conference schedule attached, which means the date is fixed before the content exists. Worth naming, because it drives the characteristic failure: a workshop scoped as though it were a tutorial, discovered on the morning to be three times too long.

    The golden path¶

    Measure the two things that decide whether a workshop works: what the environment costs, and how much material fits. About thirty minutes, and both numbers are worse than people expect.

    The second half is committed as drills/workshop_arithmetic.py, which computes it from the registry rather than quoting a figure. The first half is not, and cannot be: it needs a network, a stopwatch and a particular machine, and the numbers below are one run on one of them. Treat the shape as the claim and the seconds as an anecdote.

    Step 1: time the setup, in ideal conditions

    This is a quiet cloud machine with a warm package index and a fast link, which no attendee will have. Nothing checks these seconds, so do not reuse them:

    python3 -m venv .venv && . .venv/bin/activate
pip install mkdocs mkdocs-material pyyaml pytest

    venv_create_s=3
pip_upgrade_s=3
deps_install_s=14
packages_installed=34
bytes_on_disk=94697572
first_build_s=0  rc=0
total_s=21

    Twenty-one seconds. The number that matters is underneath it:

    requested=4   installed=34

    Four packages asked for, thirty-four installed. The failure surface of this workshop is thirty-four independent resolutions, not four, and every one can resolve differently on an attendee's laptop depending on their Python version, their existing site-packages, their corporate proxy, and what pip decides is newest that morning.

    Ninety-five megabytes per attendee, times a room.

    Step 2: remove the network from the room

    The mitigation is to resolve the dependency graph once, in advance, and ship the result:

    pip download --dest wheelhouse mkdocs mkdocs-material pyyaml pytest
pip install --no-index --find-links wheelhouse mkdocs mkdocs-material pyyaml pytest

    wheelhouse_build_s=2   files=33   bytes=28411989
offline_install_s=6    rc=0       packages=34

    Six seconds instead of fourteen, 28 MB on a stick or on a laptop serving the room, and, more importantly, the same thirty-four packages for everybody. The resolution happened once, on a machine you could inspect.

    That last property is the whole value. Speed is a bonus; determinism is the reason, because a workshop where two attendees have different transitive dependencies is a workshop where you debug one laptop while eighteen people watch.

    Step 3: the arithmetic that kills most workshop plans

    This guide's first-week path is ten concepts. drills/workshop_arithmetic.py reads them out of the registry, so the numbers below are the current ones rather than the ones that were true when this page was written:

      Technical writing as a craft                     8h
  Treating docs, setup and samples as one product  3h
  Docs-as-code                                     2h
  Markdown and its dialects                        2h
  Git authoring workflows for writers              4h
  Diátaxis                                         2h
  Choosing a static site generator                 3h
  MkDocs and Material                              4h
  Link checking                                    2h
  Documentation CI                                 4h
                                            total 34h

    A three-hour workshop, after setup and questions, offers perhaps two hours of hands-on time.

    two hours against the 34-hour first-week path: 6%
shortest path in the guide: domain-context, 6 concepts, 24h (8%)
concepts in this corpus of 3h or less: 48/106

    That second line is here because of a mistake. This page used to call first-week the shortest path in the guide, which it was, until a six-concept domain path was added and took the title by ten hours. The claim was prose, the answer was in concepts.yaml, and nothing connected them, so the page was wrong for as long as it took somebody to compute it. The drill now prints the shortest path by name and a test compares it to this block, which is the only reason the sentence you are reading is true.

    Note which direction the correction goes. The shortest path in the guide is twenty-four hours, so two hours of hands-on time is eight percent of it rather than six. The superlative was wrong and the argument got worse.

    A three-hour workshop covers about one step of a ten-step path. Not a compressed version of the path. One step of it.

    Most workshop abstracts promise the path. The abstract is written in June for a conference in October, it says "learn to build and ship a documentation pipeline", and in October somebody tries to deliver thirty-four hours in three.

    Step 4: the design consequence

    If a workshop is one step, the only question worth asking is which step needs a room, and the answer is not the most important step.

    Needs a room:   failure is confusing, recovery needs judgment, and
                seeing the person next to you helps
Does not:       mechanical, well documented, succeeds first time

    On this path, "Documentation CI" needs a room, because its failures are environmental and a stuck attendee cannot read their way out. "Markdown and its dialects" does not, however foundational, because a page explains it perfectly and nobody gets stuck.

    That inverts the usual instinct, which is to start at step 1 and get as far as possible. Starting at step 1 spends the room's scarce resource, a person who can unblock you, on the material least likely to need one.

    Step 5: what to do with the other nine steps

    Send them. A workshop that covers one step well and hands out a path for the rest is honest; a workshop that gallops through ten produces twenty people with a broken environment and a folder they will not reopen.

    The handout is the artifact that survives the room, which is the argument for having the written path exist first. A workshop built on top of a curriculum is an afternoon's work. A workshop that is the curriculum gets rebuilt every time it is delivered.

    What you built. A measured setup cost of 34 packages and 95 MB per attendee, an offline wheelhouse making it deterministic in six seconds, and the arithmetic showing a three-hour workshop is one step of a ten-step path.

    Reference¶

    A three-hour block, honestly budgeted¶

    Minutes Spent on Notes
    20 Environment, including the two people it fails for Always; plan it rather than absorb it
    10 What we are building and why Shorter than you want it to be
    100 The hands-on material The entire teaching budget
    20 Questions, and the slowest finishers Not optional
    30 Overrun nobody planned Something will go wrong

    The last row is the one people leave out, and it is why workshops overrun, rather than any individual mistake inside them.

    Environment strategies¶

    Strategy Cost in the room Determinism Fails when
    "Install these four packages" 15 to 40 minutes None Always, for somebody
    Wheelhouse on a stick or local server Under a minute High Someone has the wrong Python
    Container image, pre-pulled Under a minute Very high Docker is absent or disallowed
    Hosted environment per attendee Zero Total The venue wifi dies
    Attendees set up beforehand Zero, for the prepared None Half of them do not

    The last row is the plan most workshops actually have, and the honest reading is that it is not a plan. Pair it with one of the others.

    What needs a room¶

    Needs it Does not
    Failure modes that are confusing Anything that works first time
    Judgment calls with no single right answer Mechanical procedure
    Work people will not start alone Work they will happily read
    Anything where watching a neighbour helps Reference material
    Material where you need to watch them Material you already have feedback on

    The bottom-right cell is the trap: well-understood material is the most comfortable thing to teach and the least worth the room.

    Workshop against the alternatives¶

    Tutorial page Screencast Workshop
    Pace The reader's The video's The slowest attendee's
    Scales Indefinitely Indefinitely 20 to 30
    Cost per delivery Nothing Nothing A day
    Shows where people stick No No Yes, immediately
    Survives the day Yes Yes Only the handout does

    The fourth row is why workshops earn their cost even when the teaching is mediocre, and the fifth is why the handout matters more than the slides.

    Failure modes¶

    Failure Prevention
    Setup eats an hour Step 2, plus a greeter who starts people early
    Scoped as a path, delivered as a sprint Step 3's arithmetic, before the abstract
    One broken laptop stops the room A spare hosted environment, and permission to move on
    Everyone finishes at a different time A stated stretch task for the fast
    Nobody asks anything A planted checkpoint question, not an invitation
    The material exists only as slides Write the path first

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with the arithmetic, because it is the thing nobody has done. "A three-hour workshop has about two hours of hands-on time, and the shortest path through my curriculum is thirty-four hours. So a workshop is one step of a ten-step path rather than a compressed version of it, and the design question becomes which step needs a room, not how fast I can go."

    The follow-up is usually environments, and this is where the numbers are. I asked for four packages and got thirty-four installed, 95 MB per attendee: thirty-four independent resolutions that can differ on somebody's laptop. So I resolve once in advance and ship a wheelhouse, 28 MB, six seconds, offline, and the same thirty-four packages for everybody. Speed is a bonus; determinism is the reason, because the alternative is debugging one laptop while eighteen people watch.

    The non-obvious design point. The step that needs the room is rarely the most important one. It is the one where failure is confusing and recovery needs judgment. Foundational material that a page explains perfectly is the most comfortable thing to teach and the worst use of the only resource a workshop has.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. A workshop costs a day and reaches twenty people; a page costs a day and reaches thousands. The honest justification is not teaching efficiency, it is that a room is the only place I get to watch people fail, and that is feedback no analytics produce. A team running one workshop a quarter purely to collect it, and publishing a page from what it learned, would be making the right trade.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A wheelhouse or image that installs offline, a timed run of it, and a workshop plan whose hands-on minutes are written next to the hours of material it claims to cover.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run pip download against your own workshop's dependencies and count the files. Write your prediction down first; the gap between four and thirty-four is this page's whole subject.
    2. Take your next workshop abstract and divide its promised scope by two hours of hands-on time. A ratio much over one means the abstract is already wrong, and it is far cheaper to fix in June than in October.
    3. Ask which step of your material genuinely needs a person in the room. If the answer is "all of it", the material has not been written down yet, and writing it is the actual task.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. The setup measurements were executed: 34 packages and 94,697,572 bytes from four requested, a wheelhouse of 33 files and 28 MB built in two seconds, an offline install in six. The path arithmetic comes from this guide's own registry. What has not happened is a workshop, so the claims about what needs a room are reasoning from the design rather than observation, and the demonstrated page records workshops as having no demonstration in either repository.
