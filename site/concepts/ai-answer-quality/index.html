    Measuring AI answer quality¶

    Retrieval quality is a ceiling on answer quality, and when a wider context window stops helping, the failures left are vocabulary rather than ranking.

    core · difficulty 3 · 5h

    Asked for by name

    NVIDIA: "AI answer quality"

    Prerequisites: Output evaluation, Documentation analytics

    What it is¶

    Measuring whether an assistant answering from your corpus answers correctly. NVIDIA lists it as a metric the role owns, which makes documentation partly responsible for a system it does not control: the model is someone else's, the prompt is often someone else's, and the only input the documentation team owns is the corpus.

    That constraint is clarifying rather than limiting, because the corpus sets a ceiling. If the retrieved context does not contain the fact, a correct answer requires the model to supply it from elsewhere, which is the behaviour you are trying to prevent. Retrieval quality is an upper bound on grounded answer quality, and the bound is measurable without a model in the loop at all.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because readers have stopped arriving at documentation and started arriving at answers about it.

    A corpus is now consumed by assistants more often than many teams realise, and the failure mode is new: a confident, fluent, wrong answer, delivered without the reader ever seeing the page that would have corrected it. Link checking, sample execution and prose linting say nothing about this. The page can be perfect and unretrieved, and the answer is still wrong.

    The second reason is defensive. When an assistant answers wrongly from your documentation, the question in the room is whether the docs were wrong or the model was. Without a measurement, that argument is settled by whoever is more senior. With one, it is settled by the number.

    The golden path¶

    Measure the ceiling, find the point at which more context stops helping, and fix what is left. About forty minutes, on the companion repository's twenty-case golden set.

    Step 1: the ceiling, at one chunk

    Each golden case declares the terms a grounded answer must cite. Check whether the top retrieved chunk contains them:

    hay = f"{top.breadcrumb}\n{top.text}".lower()
missing = tuple(t for t in case.must_contain if t.lower() not in hay)

    grounded@1   16/20  (80%)

    That is not an answer-quality score. It is a statement that for four of twenty questions, a model answering from the single best chunk would have to invent the key fact or decline.

    Step 2: the ceiling across the window a model actually sees

    No real system passes one chunk. Measure the same property across the top k:

      grounded@1    16/20  (80%)
  grounded@3    18/20  (90%)
  grounded@5    18/20  (90%)
  grounded@10   18/20  (90%)

    Ten points between one chunk and three, then nothing at all out to ten.

    That plateau is the most useful shape in this measurement. While widening the window still helps, the failures are ranking failures, and a reranker or a better embedding model would address them. Once it stops helping, the remaining failures are not retrieval failures at all, and no amount of model or infrastructure will touch them.

    Step 3: look at what survived

    ungrounded at k=10: 2
   chunk-size      missing ['size']   'how do I make the chunks smaller'
   prose-linting   missing ['vale']   'how do I stop people writing hedge words'

    Both are vocabulary gaps. In each, the reader's everyday word was absent from the page that documents the thing, while the page used a precise term the reader had no reason to know. The golden set had even said so in its own why field for one of them: "the reader knows the symptom and not the tool name".

    Neither is a ranking problem, which is why k=10 could not help.

    Step 4: fix the corpus, not the retriever

    One sentence per page, each putting the reader's word beside the corpus's word. Two sentences in total, no code, no model, no reranker:

                 before   after
hit@1          60%     65%
hit@3          95%     95%
MRR          0.742   0.775
routing        70%     75%
grounded@1     80%     90%
grounded@3     90%    100%
headroom      +15%    +10%

    Four of five scores moved, and the ceiling reached 100 per cent at three chunks. The narrowing headroom is the subtle part: routing headroom measures accuracy sitting unclaimed behind a query router, and some of that gap turned out never to have been a routing problem. It was a word the corpus did not use.

    A ten-point hit@1 gap looks like a business case for a reranker. Five points of it were a synonym.

    Step 5: the limit, stated plainly

    Everything above measures retrieval. No model answered a single question.

    So the honest claim is a bound: at three chunks of context, every question in this set can be answered from material that contains the required facts. Which is not the same as saying the answers would be right. A model can misread a correct chunk, blend two chunks into a claim neither makes, or answer correctly from its own pre-training while the retrieved context is irrelevant, and that last case scores as success on every metric here.

    Measuring the rest needs a model in the loop and a grader, which is output evaluation, and it costs per run in a way this does not. The division worth keeping is that retrieval grounding is cheap, deterministic and a hard ceiling, so it is where to start and where most of the early wins are.

    What you built. A grounding ceiling measured across the context window, a plateau that distinguishes ranking failures from vocabulary failures, a two-sentence corpus fix that moved four metrics, and an explicit statement of what the measurement does not cover.

    Reference¶

    The three layers, and who owns each¶

    Layer Measures Owned by Cost per run
    Retrieval grounding Could a correct answer be built Documentation Near zero
    Answer correctness Was it built correctly The system A model call per case
    Answer usefulness Did it help the reader The product A human per case

    NVIDIA's bullet names the second and a documentation role can only move the first, which is a reasonable division: the first is a ceiling on the second.

    Metrics worth running, in order of cost¶

    Metric Needs a model Catches
    grounded@1 No The best chunk lacks the fact
    grounded@k No Whether more context would help
    hit@k, MRR No Ranking quality
    Routing accuracy No The right page of the wrong kind
    Citation validity No A cited chunk that does not support the claim
    Faithfulness grading Yes Fluent answers the context does not support
    Refusal rate on absent content Yes Inventing what the corpus lacks
    Human preference A person Everything, slowly

    The first four are free and deterministic. Teams routinely start at the bottom of this table because that is what the tooling advertises, and leave the free ceiling unmeasured.

    Why grounding and ranking have to be read together¶

    Neither metric contains the other. Cross them over the twenty cases:

                     grounded   ungrounded
  hit@1             13          0
  miss               5          2

  the two metrics agree on 15 of 20 cases (75%)

    grounded@1 is 90 per cent and hit@1 is 65, so grounding looks like the healthier number, and the top-right cell explains why that is misleading. Five cases are grounded while retrieving the wrong page: a chunk from a page the question was not about still happened to contain every term the answer must cite. An answer built on it could be correct and cite the wrong source.

    The empty cell is the one to watch over time. "Right page, wrong section" was occupied before the vocabulary fix in step 4, by the case whose top chunk came from the correct page's filtering section rather than its sizing section. A reader of hit@1 alone would have scored that case a success.

    Cell Means Visible to
    hit, grounded Working Either metric
    hit, ungrounded Right page, wrong section grounded@1 only
    miss, grounded Right terms, wrong source hit@1 only
    miss, ungrounded Nothing useful retrieved Either metric

    Two of those four failures are invisible to one of the two metrics, which is the argument for reporting both and the reason a single headline retrieval number is not worth having.

    Reading the grounded@k curve¶

    Shape Means Do
    Rises steadily with k Ranking is the bottleneck Rerank, or improve the index
    Jumps then plateaus A few ranking wins, then content gaps Fix the content
    Flat and low from k=1 The corpus lacks the material Write it
    100% at k=1 The set is too easy Write harder cases

    The second row is the common case and the one people misdiagnose, because the early jump suggests retrieval work is paying off right up to the point where it stops.

    What inflates these numbers¶

    Hazard Effect
    A golden question quoted in the corpus That page wins its own test case
    Terms chosen after seeing the retrieval Grounding checks for what you know is there
    must_contain terms that are too generic Any chunk satisfies them
    A set tuned until it goes green Measures nothing
    A model's own knowledge of the subject Scores as retrieval success

    The first is not hypothetical: adding a documentation example that quoted one golden question verbatim cost five points of hit@1, and the note explaining the mistake quoted it again and cost the same five points.

    What this cannot tell you¶

    Question Needs
    Was the answer correct A model and a grader
    Did it hedge when it should have refused A model and absent-content cases
    Did it cite the chunk it actually used Instrumentation in the answering system
    Would a reader have been satisfied A reader
    Is the assistant better than the search box Both, measured side by side

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with the ceiling, because it reframes the whole problem. "The model and the prompt usually are not mine, and the corpus is. If the retrieved context does not contain the fact, a correct answer means the model supplied it from somewhere else, which is the thing I am trying to prevent. So retrieval grounding is an upper bound on grounded answer quality, and I can measure that bound with no model in the loop and no cost per run."

    The measurement worth describing is the curve, not the number. Grounding at one chunk was 80 per cent, at three 90, and then flat all the way to ten. That plateau is diagnostic: while widening the window still helps, the failures are ranking failures and a reranker would address them; once it stops helping, the failures are not retrieval failures and no model or infrastructure will touch them.

    Then the finding, which is the part I would want to be asked about. Both surviving failures were vocabulary gaps: the reader's everyday word was missing from the page that documents the thing, while the page used a precise term the reader had no reason to know. The fix was one sentence per page. It moved hit@1 five points, MRR from 0.742 to 0.775, routing five points, grounding at one chunk from 80 to 90 per cent and at three chunks to 100, and it narrowed the routing headroom, which means part of what looked like a routing opportunity was never a routing problem. A ten-point gap looked like a case for a reranker and five points of it was a synonym.

    Volunteer the limit before anyone has to ask for it. No model answered a single question in any of this. I can say that at three chunks every question in the set can be answered from material containing the required facts. I cannot say the answers would be right: a model can misread a correct chunk, blend two into a claim neither makes, or answer correctly from pre-training while the retrieved context is irrelevant, and that last case scores as success on every metric I have. Closing that needs a model and a grader, which is output evaluation and costs per run.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A golden set with must_contain terms, a grounded@k curve over your real retriever, and a commit where fixing the corpus moved the numbers.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Compute grounded@k for k of 1, 3, 5 and 10 on a corpus you own. Predict where it plateaus. The plateau tells you whether to buy a reranker or write two sentences, and that is a budget decision.
    2. Take the cases still failing at k=10 and look for the reader's word on the page. In mine, both were synonyms the corpus never used.
    3. Check whether any documentation page quotes one of your evaluation questions. If it does, that page is winning its own test case.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. The grounded@k curve, the plateau, the two-sentence fix and the before-and-after table are all from docpack's twenty-case golden set and reproducible with docpack eval evals/golden.yaml; the recorded scores on its evaluating retrieval page were updated in the same commit, which is how the change shows up in a diff. What does not exist is any measurement with a model in the loop: nothing here grades an answer, there is no faithfulness scoring and no refusal testing, so the whole second layer of the table above is unmeasured and the demonstrated page records the concept as partial on that basis.
