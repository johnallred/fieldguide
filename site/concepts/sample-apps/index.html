    Sample applications and galleries¶

    A gallery is a list of promises, and the two ways it breaks are a sample that stopped working and a sample nobody needed.

    supporting · difficulty 3 · 6h

    Asked for by name

    Intel: "This role owns the developer first experience end to end across the Intel AI stack, including quickstarts, golden paths, reference documentation, tutorials, samples, and structured learning paths, by writing and maintaining developer documentation, the sample-app gallery, site navigation, and a crawlable, agent-ready front door"

    Prerequisites: Sample walkthroughs, Code-sample validation

    What it is¶

    A set of complete, runnable applications, and an index that presents them. Unlike a code sample in a page, a sample app is a standalone project a reader can clone and run; unlike a walkthrough, it is not primarily there to be read.

    Intel names the sample-app gallery as an owned artifact. The word that matters is gallery, because it is the index that creates the obligations. A gallery is a published list of promises: these things exist, they work, and they are worth your time.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because a gallery is where a corpus's maintenance debt becomes visible to readers.

    A documentation page that goes stale misleads whoever reads it. A sample app that goes stale wastes an hour of someone's afternoon before failing, and they conclude the product is broken rather than the sample. Then the gallery entry that promised it is still sitting there, with a title and a description, in a list that implies curation.

    The quantity makes it worse. Forty samples, each with its own dependencies and its own release cadence, is forty independent decay processes. Which is why this is difficulty 3 and why it depends on code-sample validation: a gallery without automated verification is a liability that grows.

    The golden path¶

    Build a gallery with a manifest, verify every entry, then ask whether the gallery covers anything readers need. About forty minutes. The second measurement is the one that changes what you build.

    Committed as drills/gallery_audit.py, and that is not a convenience here. The page below shows the manifest in full and the checker in fragments, and quotes output from a check_gallery.py whose source it never prints, against three sample programs it never writes. The output block was unreachable: a reader could follow every step and have nothing to run. The drill is the complete version, the manifest in it is the one above, and a test holds the two together.

    One row order changed when it was committed. The three zero-ticket topics used to be listed in the order a Python set happened to iterate them, which is to say in no order at all; they are now in the order the manifest introduces them. A table that cannot be regenerated is a screenshot with extra steps.

    Step 1: the gallery as a manifest, not a page

    mkdir -p gallery/samples/{hello,batch,stale} && cd gallery
cat > gallery.yaml <<'EOF'
samples:
  - id: hello
    title: Hello widget
    teaches: [install, first-call]
    entrypoint: samples/hello/main.py
    expect: "widgets: 3"
  - id: batch
    title: Batch requests
    teaches: [batching]
    entrypoint: samples/batch/main.py
    expect: "sent 2 batches"
  - id: stale
    title: Streaming responses
    teaches: [streaming]
    entrypoint: samples/stale/main.py
    expect: "streamed 10 chunks"
  - id: missing
    title: Webhooks
    teaches: [webhooks]
    entrypoint: samples/missing/main.py
    expect: "received 1 webhook"
EOF

    The manifest is the gallery. The page that readers see is generated from it, which means the index cannot list something the manifest does not describe, and every claim in it is checkable. teaches and expect are the two fields that make the rest of this page possible.

    Step 2: verify every entry

    for s in gallery["samples"]:
    entry = Path(s["entrypoint"])
    if not entry.exists():
        problems.append(f"{s['id']}: entrypoint {entry} does not exist")
        continue
    r = subprocess.run([sys.executable, str(entry)],
                       capture_output=True, text=True, timeout=30)
    if r.returncode != 0:
        problems.append(f"{s['id']}: exited {r.returncode}")
    elif s["expect"] not in r.stdout:
        problems.append(f"{s['id']}: expected {s['expect']!r}, got {r.stdout.strip()!r}")

    python3 check_gallery.py

    GALLERY  stale: expected 'streamed 10 chunks', got 'streamed 8 chunks'
GALLERY  missing: entrypoint samples/missing/main.py does not exist
FAIL: 2/4 samples verified
exit=1

    Two defect classes, and they are different in kind.

    stale is drift: the sample runs and its behaviour changed, which only an output assertion catches, exactly as on the code-sample validation page. missing is worse: a gallery entry with a title, a description and no code. Somebody listed a sample they intended to write.

    Half the gallery was a promise it could not keep, and the index gave no sign.

    Step 3: ask what the gallery covers

    A verified gallery can still be useless. Cross the teaches tags against what readers ask about.

    The ticket counts below are invented, because this project has no support queue. They stand in for the one input this measurement cannot be run without, and they are the shape such a distribution reliably has: a long head of failure-mode questions and a tail of feature questions. Substitute your own before drawing any conclusion from the arithmetic.

    covered = {t for s in gallery["samples"] for t in s["teaches"]}
#: Stand-in for a real support queue. Replace with yours.
asked_about = {"retries": 41, "auth": 33, "error-handling": 28,
               "pagination": 12, "batching": 6, "streaming": 3}

    topic             in gallery  tickets
retries                    -       41
auth                       -       33
error-handling             -       28
pagination                 -       12
batching                 yes        6
streaming                yes        3
install                  yes        0
first-call               yes        0
webhooks                 yes        0

tickets about uncovered topics: 114 of 123

    On that stand-in distribution, 114 of 123 tickets concern topics with no sample. The gallery covers install, first call and webhooks, which generate no tickets at all, and covers none of retries, auth, error handling or pagination. The arithmetic is only as good as the input; what travels is the shape, and the shape is not an accident.

    That is the normal state of a gallery, and the reason is structural rather than negligent. Samples get written when a feature ships, so the gallery documents the happy path of each feature and nothing about the failure modes readers hit in production. Retries and error handling are not features; they are what happens when features meet reality, so nobody is assigned to demonstrate them.

    Step 4: what the two measurements tell you to do

    verified: 2/4
demand coverage: 9 of 123 tickets

    Those numbers point in different directions, and both are actionable. The verification failure says fix or delete two entries today. The coverage gap says the next sample you write should be about retries, not about the feature that shipped last week.

    Deleting is the underrated half. A gallery of ten samples that all work and cover the top three support topics beats forty where six are broken, and missing should be removed from the manifest rather than left as an aspiration.

    What you built. A manifest whose claims are checked, a verification run that found half the gallery wanting, and a demand measurement that says what to write next.

    Reference¶

    Sample app against the alternatives¶

    In-page sample Walkthrough Sample app
    Length Lines A page A project
    Read or run Read Read, then run Run, then read
    Dependencies The docs build The docs build Its own
    Decay rate Slow Slow Fast, independently
    Verified by Sample validation Same Its own CI job
    Right for One API call One task end to end An architecture

    A sample app earns its cost when the thing being shown is a shape: how the pieces fit, how configuration is layered, what a real project's structure looks like. For anything smaller, an in-page sample is cheaper to maintain and likelier to be read.

    The manifest fields that earn their place¶

    Field Enables
    entrypoint Verification; without it the gallery cannot be checked
    expect Catching drift rather than only breakage
    teaches The coverage measurement in step 3
    stack or requires Filtering, and a reproducibility check
    updated or verified Freshness, per sample
    status Permits a published "unmaintained" state

    teaches is the one most galleries omit and the one that makes the gallery analysable. Without it you cannot answer whether the gallery covers anything, only whether it runs.

    How galleries actually fail¶

    Failure Cause Detected by
    A sample no longer runs Dependency or API change Running it in CI
    A sample runs and lies Behaviour drifted An output assertion
    A listed sample does not exist Someone listed an intention Entrypoint check
    The gallery covers nothing readers need Samples follow feature launches The demand measurement
    Forty samples, six broken No per-sample ownership All of the above, plus retirement
    Each sample pins differently No shared environment policy Reproducible environments

    Running them in CI without the bill¶

    Strategy Cost
    Every sample on every pull request Prohibitive past a handful
    Nightly, all samples Good default; failures open issues
    On pull request, only samples touching changed paths Fast and partial
    Weekly, with dependency updates Catches upstream drift specifically
    Smoke only: it starts and exits 0 Cheap, and misses drift

    Nightly plus output assertions is the usual right answer. Smoke testing a gallery is the common choice and it catches only the first failure row above.

    Retiring a sample¶

    A gallery needs a documented way to remove an entry, or it only grows. Retirement applies: remove it from the manifest, leave a redirect if it had a URL, and say why. A sample marked unmaintained in a gallery is more honest than one quietly broken, and far more honest than one listed and absent.

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with the gallery as a promise list. "A sample app that goes stale costs a reader an hour and they conclude the product is broken, not the sample. So the gallery is a manifest rather than a page: each entry declares an entrypoint, an expected output and what it teaches, the page is generated from it, and CI runs every one."

    The follow-up is coverage, and this is the stronger half. A verified gallery can still be useless. Crossing the teaches tags against support volume on my own worked example puts 114 of 123 tickets on topics with no sample. I should be clear that those ticket counts are invented, since I have no support queue; the reason to expect that shape is structural. Samples get written when features ship, so a gallery documents each feature's happy path and nothing about retries, auth or error handling. Those are not features, so nobody is assigned to demonstrate them.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Every sample is an independent maintenance commitment with its own dependency tree, and the honest consequence is that the right number of samples is much smaller than teams want. Ten that work and cover the top support topics beat forty with six broken, and getting from forty to ten means deleting, which needs a retirement policy nobody wants to write.

    One sharper point. The entrypoint check is the cheapest finding in this whole area. A gallery entry with a title, a description and no code is the easiest thing in the world to create, because the index and the implementation live in different places. One Path.exists() per entry catches it, and on an old gallery it usually catches something.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A gallery driven by a manifest, a CI job that runs every entry and asserts its output, and a coverage measurement against support volume.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run the golden path. Step 2's entrypoint check takes a minute to write and finds real things.
    2. Tag your existing samples with what they teach, then rank your support topics by volume. The gap is your backlog, and it will not look like your roadmap.
    3. Pick the oldest sample nobody has verified and run it. Whatever happens is information.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Neither of this guide's repositories ships a sample gallery, which the demonstrated page records; docpack validates samples embedded in its own documentation, which is the in-page case rather than this one. What travels is one real measurement and one method: half the gallery was unverifiable, which the entrypoint and output checks found, and the coverage cross-tabulation is a method whose ticket counts here are invented and have to come from your own queue.
