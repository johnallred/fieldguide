    Real-time and graphics systems¶

    The median completion time barely moved while the deadline miss rate went from zero to thirty per cent, and then the system fell off a cliff.

    context · difficulty 2 · 4h

    Asked for by name

    NVIDIA: "Documentation experience in graphics, simulation, robotics, or real-time systems"

    What it is¶

    Systems where being late is being wrong. A real-time system is not a fast system; it is one with a deadline, and a result delivered after the deadline has no value, or negative value. Graphics is the familiar case: a frame that misses the display refresh is not a slightly slower frame, it is a dropped frame, and the user sees a stutter rather than a delay.

    That one property changes which numbers matter, and it is the thing documentation in this domain most often gets wrong.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because the metric conventions a documentation writer brings from web services are actively misleading here, and NVIDIA lists four domains that all share this property.

    In a web service, a slow request is a degraded request and the mean is a reasonable summary. In a real-time system, the mean is close to irrelevant: a system that meets its deadline 99 per cent of the time and misses one per cent has a one per cent failure rate, and the mean will look excellent either way. Writing "average frame time 8 ms" about a 60 Hz target is technically true and tells the reader nothing about whether their frames land.

    The golden path¶

    Run the same work against tightening deadlines and watch the summary statistics stay healthy while the system fails. About twenty-five minutes, pure Python, no hardware.

    Step 1: a periodic task, and the work it does

    for i in range(N):
    target = start + (i + 1) * period / 1000
    now = time.perf_counter()
    if target > now:
        time.sleep(target - now)
    sum(x * x for x in range(WORK))            # the work
    done.append((time.perf_counter() - target) * 1000)

    the work itself: mean 2.068 ms per cycle

    About two milliseconds of work. Now give it less and less room.

    Step 2: sweep the deadline

    One run, on a shared two-core cloud machine:

      period  misses        completion time (ms)
    (ms)              mean     p50     p99     max
    10.0     0/250    2.363   1.762   4.314   4.551
     5.0     0/250    1.702   1.521   3.108   4.332
     2.0    67/250    3.308   1.482  22.912  24.702
     1.5   250/250   39.945  42.665  47.797  49.909

    Read the p50 column down the first three rows: 1.762, 1.521, 1.482 ms. It does not rise. It improves.

    Now read the miss column beside it: 0, then 0, then 67 of 250.

    A quarter of deadlines missed, and the median is better than it was on the row with no misses at all. A dashboard plotting median completion time would show a healthy, improving line. The mean rises from 2.4 to 3.3 ms, which nobody pages anyone about. The columns that noticed are p99, which went to 22.9 ms, fifteen times the median, and the miss count.

    Step 3: the numbers move, and the shape does not

    Repeating the sweep is itself instructive. At the 2 ms period, across six runs:

    misses at period 2.0 ms:  67, 81, 164, 176, 202, 205  (of 250)

    A factor of three, from nothing but what else the machine was doing. So the specific counts above are one sample and not a property of the code, which is worth saying before quoting any of them.

    Three things were stable across every run:

    a loose deadline (10 ms)   0 misses, always
a tight one (1 ms)         250/250, always
the median                 stayed between 1.4 and 4 ms throughout

    The median never tracked the miss rate, in any run. That is the transferable finding, and the variance is the second one: a general-purpose machine cannot give a repeatable answer here, which is precisely why real-time work reaches for a dedicated kernel, pinned cores and locked memory rather than better averages.

    Step 4: the cliff

    Wherever the transition falls, it is not gradual:

      2.0 ms period:   67/250 missed,  p50  1.5 ms
  1.5 ms period:  250/250 missed,  p50 42.7 ms

    Completion time goes from 1.5 ms to 42.7 ms, a factor of 28, for a 25 per cent tightening of the deadline. Once the work cannot fit in the period, each cycle starts late, so the next starts later, and lag compounds without limit. There is no gentle region: the system is fine, then it is unrecoverable.

    That is the characteristic failure shape of this whole domain, and it is why headroom is discussed differently here. A web service at 80 per cent utilisation is busy. A real-time system at 80 per cent utilisation is one scheduling hiccup from the cliff.

    Step 5: what this means for the writing

    Three consequences, each a concrete documentation decision:

    state the deadline, not the average      "16.6 ms at 60 Hz", never "fast"
publish the miss rate                    the mean cannot express failure
publish the tail, p99 and max            the mean hides a 15 ms max at p50 1.5

    And one more that is easy to miss. In the run above, the work took 2.07 ms and median completion at a 10 ms period was 1.76 ms, which looks impossible. They are different statistics, a mean against a median, on a machine whose timings vary by a factor of three. That is the lesson rather than a footnote: on this hardware the overhead of being scheduled is not separable from the noise, so any budget a page offers has to be stated with the margin included and the measurement conditions named.

    What you built. A deadline sweep showing a median that improves while a quarter of deadlines are missed, a variance measurement showing the counts are not reproducible, the cliff where lag starts compounding, and the things a page in this domain has to publish instead of an average.

    Reference¶

    Hard, firm and soft¶

    Kind A missed deadline Example
    Hard System failure, possibly unsafe Flight control, motor commutation
    Firm The result is worthless, no harm A dropped video frame
    Soft Value degrades with lateness A UI animation, a game's physics

    Most things called real-time are firm or soft. The distinction matters in writing because it decides whether the correct advice is "guarantee it" or "degrade gracefully", and those are different pages.

    Which numbers to publish¶

    Statistic Says Verdict here
    Mean Typical throughput Nearly useless; see step 2
    Median Typical experience Actively misleading; it was flat at 30% misses
    p99, p99.9 The tail that fails Essential
    Max over a run The worst case observed Essential, and not a bound
    Deadline miss rate The failure rate The number that matters
    Jitter, max minus min Predictability Often the real requirement
    Worst-case execution time A provable bound Hard systems only, and expensive

    A page that quotes a mean in this domain has chosen the one statistic that cannot express the failure mode.

    Frame budgets, as the familiar case¶

    Refresh Budget per frame
    30 Hz 33.3 ms
    60 Hz 16.6 ms
    90 Hz 11.1 ms
    120 Hz 8.3 ms
    144 Hz 6.9 ms

    The budget covers everything: simulation, culling, draw submission, present, and the scheduling overhead from step 4. Documentation that gives a per-feature cost without saying what the budget is leaves the reader unable to add up.

    Why a general-purpose OS is not real-time¶

    Source of lateness Typical scale
    Scheduler granularity Tens of microseconds to milliseconds
    Another process being runnable Unbounded
    Page faults and memory allocation Microseconds to milliseconds
    Garbage collection, where present Milliseconds, unpredictably
    Interrupt handling Microseconds
    Frequency and thermal management Milliseconds, and invisible

    Measured here: the same sweep gave between 67 and 205 deadline misses out of 250 across six runs, a factor of three from nothing but neighbouring load. A page claiming a sub-millisecond guarantee on a stock kernel is claiming something the kernel does not offer, and this is why real-time work reaches for PREEMPT_RT, core pinning, locked memory and allocation-free hot paths.

    Terms worth recognising¶

    Term Means
    Jitter Variation in timing, often worse than latency
    Determinism The same input gives the same timing
    Priority inversion A low-priority task blocking a high-priority one
    Starvation A task that never gets scheduled
    Admission control Refusing work to protect the deadline
    Headroom Unused capacity, which is the safety margin
    WCET Worst-case execution time, a proved upper bound

    Things to put in a page in this domain¶

    Do Instead of
    State the deadline in milliseconds "Real-time", "fast", "low latency"
    Publish a miss rate A mean
    Give the tail and the max A single number
    Include scheduling overhead in budgets Quoting the work alone
    Say what degrades when the deadline is missed Leaving the failure mode implicit
    Name the required kernel or configuration Implying stock hardware suffices

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with the definition, because it is the whole thing. "A real-time system is not a fast system, it is one with a deadline, and a result after the deadline has no value. A frame that misses the refresh is not a slow frame, it is a dropped frame. That changes which numbers mean anything."

    Then the measurement, which is the part worth bringing. I ran a fixed piece of work against tightening periods. At a 10 ms deadline, no misses and a median of 1.76 ms. At 2 ms, sixty-seven misses out of 250 and a median of 1.48, better than the row with no misses, while p99 went to 22.9 ms, fifteen times the median. A quarter of deadlines missed and every summary statistic looking healthy or improving.

    The second finding, which I would not have had without repeating it. The miss count at that period ranged from 67 to 205 across six runs, a factor of three from neighbouring load alone. So the counts are not a property of the code, and the transferable claim is the shape: the median never tracked the miss rate in any run. The variance is itself the argument for why this domain uses dedicated kernels and pinned cores rather than better averages.

    The third, which is about headroom. Tightening the deadline by 25 per cent, from 2 ms to 1.5, took it from sixty-seven misses to all 250 and the median from 1.5 ms to 42.7. Once the work does not fit, every cycle starts later than the last and lag compounds, so there is no gentle region. That is why utilisation reads differently here: 80 per cent in a web service is busy, and in a real-time system it is one hiccup from the cliff.

    Be honest about the depth. This is Python on a general-purpose kernel, which is the right vehicle for showing why general-purpose kernels are not real-time and the wrong one for anything else. No PREEMPT_RT, no GPU, no frame capture, no graphics pipeline. The reasoning about statistics and budgets transfers; the engineering does not, and context tier is the honest claim.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A deadline sweep over a workload of yours, reported as a miss rate and a tail rather than an average.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run the sweep in step 2. Predict the period at which misses begin, then look at what the median does on either side of it.
    2. Take any latency claim in your own documentation and work out whether it could be true while the system fails a third of the time. If the claim is a mean or a median, it can.
    3. Add the scheduling or framework overhead to one published cost figure. On an idle machine it was a third again here, and readers budget with the number you give them.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. The sweep ran on this machine and the script is a dozen lines, so the flat median against a 30 per cent miss rate and the cliff between 2.0 and 1.5 ms are both reproducible. What is absent is the domain's actual engineering: no real-time kernel, no graphics pipeline, no frame profiler, no hardware timing. The demonstrated page records this as the statistics rather than the systems work.
