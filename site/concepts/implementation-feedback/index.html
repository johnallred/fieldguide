    Giving architecture owners implementation feedback¶

    Reporting what broke when you built someone else's design, in a form they can act on.

    core · difficulty 2 · 3h

    sharing implementation feedback (what works, breaks, and scales) to refine content models, information architecture, and AI-readiness patterns

    Source: nvidia posting, verbatim

    Prerequisites: A systems mindset

    What it is¶

    Telling the person who designed a standard what happened when you applied it at scale, specifically enough that they can change the design. Not escalating a complaint, and not quietly working around it.

    The useful unit is an instance with a count: this rule, this many pages, this failure, this proposal.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    This is the most distinctive thing in the NVIDIA posting and the easiest to skim past. Read the two bullets together:

    "Collaborate with documentation leads on knowledge architecture, sharing implementation feedback (what works, breaks, and scales) to refine content models, information architecture, and AI-readiness patterns."

    "A collaborative style: you turn design intent into working systems and give leads clear, constructive feedback grounded in implementation."

    The role is explicitly the implementer advising the architects. Documentation leads own the content model; you apply it to thousands of pages and find out where it does not hold. That split is deliberate, and it means the job has a built-in feedback obligation. You are not expected to accept a standard you have discovered is wrong, and you are not authorised to change it unilaterally either.

    On a Tuesday this is a specific recurring moment. The content model requires an audience field with four allowed values. You apply it across the Omniverse corpus and find 180 pages that are genuinely for two audiences at once, and another 40 where the honest answer is a fifth value nobody anticipated. You can pick the closest value and move on, which corrupts the data quietly. You can block on it, which stops the migration. Or you can report it in a form the lead can decide from, which is the job.

    The reason this is core: the quality of this feedback loop determines whether the standards improve or ossify. A lead who only hears "done" learns nothing from a migration that touched three thousand pages.

    The golden path¶

    Turn a real friction into a report someone can decide from. Thirty minutes, and the artifact is the deliverable.

    Step 1: hit the friction and resist both reflexes

    mkdir -p feedback-drill && cd feedback-drill
cat > friction.md <<'EOF'
THE STANDARD   Every page declares exactly one `audience`, from:
               new-user | developer | operator | contributor

WHAT HAPPENED  Applying it to 260 pages:
                 - 180 pages are honestly two audiences
                 - 40 pages are for partners, which is not a value
                 - 40 pages fit cleanly

THE TWO WRONG REFLEXES
  Pick the closest value and move on.
      -> the field now contains guesses. Every downstream filter is
         subtly wrong and nobody can tell.
  Block the migration and wait for a decision.
      -> three thousand pages stalled on a taxonomy question.
EOF
cat friction.md

    Step 2: get a number, because a number is what makes it a design question

    An anecdote is a complaint. A count is evidence.

    mkdir -p docs
python3 - <<'PY'
# Stand in for a real corpus scan. In practice this reads your pages and
# classifies them; what matters is that the output is counts, not opinions.
import json, pathlib
findings = {
    "total_pages": 260,
    "single_audience_clean": 40,
    "two_audiences_honestly": 180,
    "audience_not_in_vocabulary": 40,
    "examples": {
        "two_audiences": [
            "install/requirements.md  (new-user and operator)",
            "usd/schema-basics.md     (developer and contributor)",
        ],
        "missing_value": [
            "partners/certification.md",
            "partners/branding.md",
        ],
    },
}
pathlib.Path("findings.json").write_text(json.dumps(findings, indent=2))
print(json.dumps(findings, indent=2)[:400], "...")
PY

    Step 3: write the report in the shape a decision-maker can use

    Five parts, in this order. The order is the content: the proposal comes after the evidence, and the recommendation comes last so it is clearly separable from the facts.

    cat > report.md <<'EOF'
# Content model: the `audience` field does not survive the Omniverse corpus

## What I did
Applied the content model to 260 pages in `omniverse/`, the first corpus large
enough to test the closed vocabulary.

## What happened
| Outcome | Pages |
|---|---|
| One audience, cleanly | 40 |
| Honestly two audiences | 180 |
| Audience not in the vocabulary (partners) | 40 |

69% of pages cannot satisfy the field as written. Examples:
- `install/requirements.md` is read by new users and by operators, and the
  page is correct for both.
- `partners/certification.md` has no available value.

## What I think is happening
The field conflates two things: *who the page is written for* (one answer, a
real editorial decision) and *who reads it* (several answers, an observation).
The vocabulary is right for the first and is being asked to do the second.

## Options, with what each costs
| Option | Cost | Who pays |
|---|---|---|
| Allow a list of audiences | Every filter becomes a set operation; navigation needs a primary anyway | Tooling, once |
| Add `primary_audience` plus optional `also_read_by` | One more field; migration can default the second to empty | Authors, slightly |
| Add `partner` to the vocabulary | Solves 40 pages, not the 180 | Nobody, but it is a partial fix |
| Keep as is, authors pick the closest | Free today; the field silently becomes unreliable | Every downstream consumer, forever |

## What I would do
`primary_audience` plus optional `also_read_by`. It keeps the editorial
decision single-valued, which is what navigation and retrieval filters need,
and it stops authors guessing. I can migrate the 260 pages in a day with the
primary inferred from the directory and `also_read_by` left empty.

I am not blocking on this: I will hold the Omniverse migration for a week and
proceed with the other corpora, which do not hit it.
EOF
wc -l report.md

    Step 4: check it against the test that matters

    cat > test.md <<'EOF'
Can the lead decide from this without talking to me?

[x] Is there a number?                      69% of 260 pages
[x] Can they see the actual pages?          two examples per case
[x] Is my diagnosis separable from the data? "What I think is happening"
[x] Are the options costed?                  table, with who pays
[x] Is my recommendation last and labelled?  "What I would do"
[x] Do they know what happens if they say nothing?  holding one week
EOF
cat test.md

    What you built. A report that is decidable without a meeting. The last line of the checklist is the one people forget: a lead needs to know the default outcome if they do nothing, because that is what converts your report from a question into a decision with a deadline.

    Reference¶

    The five parts, and why the order matters¶

    Part Purpose
    What I did Scope, so they know how much the finding generalises
    What happened Counts and examples. No interpretation yet
    What I think is happening Your diagnosis, clearly marked as yours
    Options, costed Shows you understand the design space, not just your preference
    What I would do Last, so it is separable. A recommendation, not a conclusion

    Putting the recommendation first reads as advocacy, and a lead who disagrees with it discounts the evidence behind it. Putting it last lets them reach a different conclusion from the same facts without overruling you.

    What makes feedback actionable¶

    Actionable Not actionable
    "180 of 260 pages are two audiences" "The audience field is awkward"
    "Here are two example pages" "Lots of pages have this problem"
    "Option A costs tooling work once" "We should allow multiple values"
    "I am holding one corpus for a week" "Let me know what you think"
    "This is the first corpus large enough to test it" "This has always been a problem"

    Things worth reporting that people do not¶

    Report Why it is valuable
    A standard that worked better than expected Leads hear only about breakage and over-correct
    A rule nobody violated in 3,000 pages It may be unnecessary, and removing it is cheap
    A validator whose warnings are universally ignored A dead gate is worse than none
    The cost of a rule in author time Nobody measures this and it is the main objection
    Where the model held under scale "What works" is in the posting's own phrasing

    That last row is in the quote and is the part almost everybody drops. "What works, breaks, and scales" is three things.

    Failure modes¶

    Failure What it causes
    Silent workaround Data corruption nobody can trace. The worst one
    Blocking on every ambiguity You become the reason the migration stalled
    Reporting friction without counts Reads as a complaint, gets deferred
    Arriving with one option Reads as a demand; leads defend the status quo
    Reporting only failures The lead over-corrects a working standard
    No default if they do nothing Your report joins a backlog

    How to talk about it¶

    What a competent answer sounds like. Tell it as a report, with the number first. "The content model required one audience per page. Applying it to 260 pages, 69 percent either had two honest audiences or had none in the vocabulary. I wrote it up with the counts, example pages, three options with their costs, and my recommendation last, and I kept the other corpora moving while the lead decided. We ended up splitting the field into a primary and an optional secondary."

    The follow-up is "what if the lead disagrees with you", which is testing whether you can be overruled gracefully. The answer worth giving: implement their decision properly and instrument it. If you believed the field would become unreliable, add a check that measures how often authors pick the closest value rather than a true one, and report the number in six months. That is disagreeing with evidence rather than with insistence, and it is the only form of disagreement that survives a difference in authority.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. This kind of report costs an hour to write and it is an hour you are not migrating pages. There is a real version of this job where you write careful reports about everything and ship nothing, and a lead will notice. The filter is scale: report what you found because you did the work at volume, and absorb what is merely awkward. A finding that only appears at 260 pages is worth an hour; a rule you personally find annoying is not.

    One sharper point. The structural value of this role is that the implementer sees things the architect cannot. A content model is a hypothesis about a corpus, and the corpus is the only thing that can falsify it. Saying that out loud reframes the feedback obligation from politeness to epistemics, which is much closer to how NVIDIA's posting describes it, and it explains why they wanted this as a named requirement rather than assuming it.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. One written report in the five-part shape, about something real. It can be from any context: a schema you applied, a lint rule you rolled out, a convention you migrated to.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Find a standard you worked around rather than reported. Everyone has one. Write the report retroactively, with the count. The exercise is the point. If the count is zero, you have not looked at a wide enough window: the workaround nobody reported is the one that has been in place longest.
    2. Report something that worked. Harder than it sounds, and the rarest thing in this category. Find a rule that held up under scale and say so with a number.
    3. Find one dead gate. A check whose warnings nobody acts on. Propose removing it. Removing a rule is the clearest possible demonstration that your feedback is about the system rather than about your preferences.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. "I give constructive feedback" is unfalsifiable. A one-page report with a percentage, two example files, a costed options table and a recommendation at the bottom is a thing an interviewer can read in two minutes and immediately recognise as the output of someone who has done this.

    How this would be demonstrated elsewhere¶

    The whole concept is a conversation with someone who owns a design, so the evidence is in their repository, their review threads and their decisions, not in yours.

    Where the skill is exercised. When documenting a feature reveals that the feature is confusing, and you have to decide whether to write a clearer page or say the design is wrong. In a review comment that proposes a product change rather than a wording change. In being right about it often enough to be asked next time.

    What a learner would do. Keep a log, for a month, of every time documentation work surfaced something that was not a documentation problem:

    what I was documenting     what I found                  what I did
the retry flag             two flags do the same thing    filed a bug
the error codes            404 and 410 are inconsistent   wrote around it
the setup steps            step 3 exists for a bug        proposed removal

    Then count the third column. Most people find they wrote around nearly all of it, which is the finding: documentation work is the most reliable source of design feedback in an organisation and the least likely to be delivered as feedback, because writing around a problem is faster and feels like the job.

    What competence looks like.

    Evidence Why it carries weight
    A product change traceable to a docs review The strongest and rarest
    A bug filed from writing a page The habit, present
    A design review you were invited into Earned by prior accuracy
    Feedback given and declined, recorded without rancour Shows the relationship survived
    A page you did write around, and said why Honest about the tradeoff

    The specific skill, which is what to make checkable. Feedback lands when it is phrased as a reader's observed difficulty rather than as an opinion about the design. "This is confusing" is an opinion. "Documenting this takes four paragraphs to explain which of two flags to use, and here is the draft" is an observation with the evidence attached, and it is hard to argue with because the draft is the argument.

    Why it does not commit to a repository. A single-author project has no architecture owner to give feedback to. Even in a team, the artifact is a decision in someone else's system, and the part that demonstrates skill, that the feedback was accepted and acted on, is precisely the part you do not control. This is why the concept stays craft and why this guide claims no demonstration of it.
