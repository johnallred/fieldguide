    Grounding and citation¶

    Making an answer traceable to the text it came from, and failing it mechanically when it is not.

    core · difficulty 2 · 4h

    Practical experience applying LLMs to content workflows (prompting, retrieval and grounding, output evaluation)

    Source: nvidia posting, verbatim

    Prerequisites: RAG pipelines

    Leads to: Documentation-backed AI assistants, Hallucination and guardrails, Output evaluation

    What it is¶

    Requiring every claim in an answer to be traceable to a specific piece of retrieved text, and checking that requirement mechanically rather than trusting it. Citation is the visible half: the answer names its sources. Grounding is the enforcement: a claim with no supporting text is rejected before anyone reads it.

    The distinction matters because a citation is not evidence of grounding. A model will happily attach [c3] to a sentence c3 does not support.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    NVIDIA lists it in the middle of the three-part requirement: "prompting, retrieval and grounding, output evaluation". The ordering is the pipeline, and grounding is the stage that makes the other two auditable.

    What makes it core rather than a nicety is the asymmetry described in RAG: a generated answer hides the ranking. A reader looking at a search results page judges five results themselves. A reader looking at a paragraph has no way to tell whether it synthesised two relevant chunks or invented a bridge between two half-relevant ones. Citation restores the reader's ability to check, and mechanical grounding restores yours.

    On a Tuesday it is a specific and common defect. The assistant says "Set max_memory_mb to cap GPU memory, with a default of 16. [c3]" That sentence contains two facts from two chunks: the flag name from the legacy page and the default from the current one, fused into a claim that is true of neither. The citation is present. The answer is wrong. Only a check that tests the claim against the cited text catches it, and the check is about forty lines.

    The golden path¶

    Build a grounding checker, run it against answers that look right, and watch it reject three different kinds of ungrounded claim. Forty minutes, dependency-free.

    Step 2: the checker

    cat > ground.py <<'PY'
"""Check that every sentence in an answer is supported by its cited chunk.

Three checks, in increasing strictness, and the second is the one that earns
its keep:

  CITED       does every sentence carry a citation at all?
  SUPPORTED   do the sentence's content words appear in the cited chunk?
  NUMBERS     does every number in the sentence appear in the cited chunk?

The NUMBERS check is separate because numbers are where ungrounded answers do
the most damage and where word-overlap scoring is blindest: "the default is
16" and "the default is 24" differ by one token out of five and by everything
that matters.
"""
import json
import re
import sys

CITATION = re.compile(r"\[(\w+)\]")
STOP = {
    "the", "a", "an", "to", "is", "are", "of", "in", "on", "for", "with",
    "and", "or", "it", "its", "set", "was", "be", "by", "at", "as", "this",
    "that", "will", "can", "you", "your", "not", "from", "which",
}

#: A sentence must have at least this share of its content words present in
#: the cited chunk. Deliberately not 1.0: an answer is allowed to paraphrase
#: and to use connective words the source does not contain.
SUPPORT_THRESHOLD = 0.7


def content_words(text):
    return [
        w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9_]+", text.lower())
        if w not in STOP and len(w) > 1
    ]


def numbers(text):
    return set(re.findall(r"\b\d+(?:\.\d+)?\b", text))


#: A sentence, followed by any citations that trail it. Matching them together
#: is the whole point: splitting on `(?<=[.!?])\s+` first puts a trailing
#: `[c3]` in its own fragment and leaves the sentence it belongs to looking
#: uncited, which made the first version of this checker reject every answer.
SENTENCE = re.compile(r"(.+?[.!?])((?:\s*\[\w+\])*)", re.S)


def sentences(answer):
    """Split into (sentence, citations), with a trailing citation kept.

    A citation applies to the sentence it follows. One citation covering three
    sentences is NOT accepted, which is strict on purpose: a single citation
    spanning several claims is exactly the pattern that hides a fused answer.
    """
    out = []
    for match in SENTENCE.finditer(answer.strip()):
        text = CITATION.sub("", match.group(1)).strip()
        if text:
            out.append((text, CITATION.findall(match.group(2))))
    return out


def check(answer, context):
    findings = []
    for text, cited in sentences(answer):
        if not cited:
            findings.append(("uncited", text, "no citation on this sentence"))
            continue

        source = " ".join(context.get(c, "") for c in cited)
        missing_sources = [c for c in cited if c not in context]
        if missing_sources:
            findings.append(
                ("bad-citation", text, f"cites {missing_sources}, not in the context")
            )
            continue

        source_words = set(content_words(source))
        words = content_words(text)
        present = [w for w in words if w in source_words]
        ratio = len(present) / len(words) if words else 1.0
        if ratio < SUPPORT_THRESHOLD:
            absent = [w for w in words if w not in source_words]
            findings.append((
                "unsupported", text,
                f"{ratio:.0%} of content words in {cited}; missing {absent}",
            ))

        absent_numbers = numbers(text) - numbers(source)
        if absent_numbers:
            findings.append((
                "ungrounded-number", text,
                f"{sorted(absent_numbers)} not in {cited}",
            ))
    return findings


def main():
    context = json.load(open("context.json"))
    for answer in json.load(open("answers.json")):
        findings = check(answer["text"], context)
        verdict = "PASS" if not findings else "REJECT"
        print(f"{verdict:<7} [{answer['label']}] {answer['text']}")
        for kind, text, why in findings:
            print(f"        {kind}: {why}")
        print()
    return 0


if __name__ == "__main__":
    sys.exit(main())
PY
python3 ground.py

    Step 3: see what each check catches that the others do not

    python3 - <<'PY'
import json

from ground import check, content_words

context = json.load(open("context.json"))
source_words = set(content_words(context["c3"]))


def overlap(answer):
    words = content_words(answer.replace("[c3]", ""))
    return sum(1 for w in words if w in source_words) / len(words)


print("=== when does the NUMBERS check earn its keep? ===")
print("A wrong number is itself a missing content word, so on a SHORT")
print("sentence the support check catches it anyway. On a long one it does not.\n")

for answer in [
    "The default is 16. [c3]",
    "The default is 24. [c3]",
    "max_memory_gb caps the GPU memory the server will allocate, "
    "and the default is 16. [c3]",
    "max_memory_gb caps the GPU memory the server will allocate, "
    "and the default is 24. [c3]",
]:
    kinds = [f[0] for f in check(answer, context)] or ["none"]
    print(f"  overlap {overlap(answer):5.0%}   findings {kinds}")
    print(f"    {answer}")

print()
print("=== the fused answer, in detail ===")
fused = "Set max_memory_mb to cap GPU memory, with a default of 16. [c3]"
for kind, text, why in check(fused, context):
    print(f"  {kind}: {why}")
PY

    The four-line table is the argument, and it is narrower than the usual claim:

    Answer Overlap Fires
    short, default 16 100% nothing. Correct
    short, default 24 50% unsupported and ungrounded-number
    long, default 16 100% nothing. Correct
    long, default 24 88% ungrounded-number only

    On a short sentence a wrong number is a large share of the content words, so the support check catches it without help. On a long sentence the same fabrication sits at 88% overlap, comfortably above any usable threshold, and only the dedicated numbers check sees it. Since real answers are long sentences, that is where the check pays for itself.

    The fused answer is rejected for missing max_memory_mb and cap. The first is the finding; the second is a false positive, because c3 says "caps" and this checker does no stemming. Worth noticing: lexical support is a proxy with real noise in it, which is why the threshold is 0.7 rather than 1.0 and why it has to be set from a labelled set rather than reasoned to.

    Step 4: the prompt side, which reduces the problem rather than solving it

    cat > prompt.txt <<'EOF'
Answer the question using ONLY the numbered sources below.

Rules:
- Put a citation [id] at the end of EVERY sentence, naming the single source
  that supports that sentence.
- If a sentence needs two sources, split it into two sentences.
- Quote exact values (numbers, flag names, version strings) verbatim from the
  source. Never convert, round, or infer a value.
- If the sources do not contain the answer, reply exactly:
  "I could not find this in the documentation."
  That is a correct and expected answer.

Sources:
{context}

Question: {question}
EOF
cat prompt.txt

    What you built. A checker that rejects three kinds of ungrounded answer, and the prompt that reduces how often they occur.

    The division of labour is the lesson. The prompt makes grounded answers more likely; the checker makes ungrounded ones not ship. Only the second is a guarantee, and the "split it into two sentences" rule exists specifically to make the checker's job possible: one citation covering three claims cannot be verified, so the prompt is designed around what the validator can check.

    Reference¶

    The three failures, and which check catches each¶

    Failure Looks like Caught by
    Uncited claim A plausible sentence with no source Citation presence
    Fused claim Two true facts from two chunks, combined into a false one Word support against the cited chunk
    Invented value A number or name that is nowhere in the context The numbers check
    Wrong source A true sentence citing the wrong chunk Word support
    Dangling citation A citation to a chunk not in the context Citation resolution

    Why numbers get their own check¶

    Answer Overlap with c3 Support check Numbers check
    "…allocate, and the default is 16." 100% passes passes
    "…allocate, and the default is 24." 88% passes fails

    A wrong number is one token. In a sentence of eight content words that is 88% overlap, which sits above any threshold loose enough to allow paraphrase. A grounding check that is only a similarity score will pass fabricated numbers in exactly the answers that are long enough to sound authoritative.

    The same argument applies to flag names, version strings and error codes, which is why the checker tokenizes keeping underscores.

    Prompt rules that make checking possible¶

    Rule Why it exists
    Citation at the end of every sentence A paragraph-level citation cannot be verified per claim
    Split a sentence that needs two sources Makes fused claims structurally impossible to express
    Quote values verbatim, never convert Gives the numbers check something exact to match
    An explicit refusal string A refusal must be available, or the model fills the gap
    Sources numbered, not pasted raw The citation needs a stable label

    Notice that four of the five are written for the benefit of the validator rather than the reader. That is the right way round: design the output format so it can be checked, then check it.

    Degrees of grounding, from cheap to strict¶

    Level Check Cost
    Citation present Every sentence has an [id] trivial
    Citation resolves The id is in the context trivial
    Lexical support Content words appear in the cited chunk cheap, what this page builds
    Exact-value support Numbers and identifiers match verbatim cheap, highest value
    Entailment A model judges whether the source supports the claim expensive, needs its own evaluation
    Human review A person checks expensive, the only real ceiling

    Lexical plus exact-value is the sweet spot: it runs in CI, costs nothing, and catches the failures that matter most in documentation. Entailment checking is better and introduces a second model whose errors you now also have to measure.

    What grounding does not give you¶

    Claim Reality
    "The answer is correct" It is supported by the retrieved text. If the page is wrong, so is the answer
    "No hallucination" It bounds fabrication to what retrieval returned
    "The answer is complete" A grounded answer can omit the crucial caveat on another page
    "The citation is the right one" Lexical support is a proxy; a true sentence can cite a near-miss

    The first row is the one to say out loud. Grounding inherits the corpus's errors, so it is a check on the pipeline and not on the documentation. That is what freshness monitoring and reading code to verify are for.

    How to talk about it¶

    What a competent answer sounds like. Lead with the fused claim, because it is the failure that proves you have looked at real output. "Citations are not evidence of grounding. The failure I check for is a fused claim: the answer takes a flag name from one chunk and a default value from another and combines them into a sentence that is true of neither, with a citation attached. So every sentence carries exactly one citation, and a checker verifies the sentence's content words and every number against that specific chunk. The numbers check is separate, because a fabricated default inside a long sentence sits at 88% word overlap, which is above any threshold loose enough to allow paraphrase."

    The follow-up is "what is your threshold and how did you pick it", and the honest answer is that a fixed word-overlap threshold is crude and you pick it from a labelled set. Too strict and legitimate paraphrase gets rejected, which trains people to disable the check; too loose and fused claims pass. The threshold is a parameter you measure, not a number you reason to, which is why this concept leads directly into output evaluation.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Strict grounding makes answers worse to read. A citation at the end of every sentence is noisy, the one-source-per-sentence rule forbids useful synthesis, and a model constrained this hard produces prose that reads like a quotation anthology. There is a real tension between an answer that is pleasant and an answer that is checkable, and for developer documentation checkable wins, because a wrong command costs more than an awkward paragraph. For a general-audience assistant that trade might go the other way.

    One sharper point. The strongest observation is that grounding is the mechanism that makes an assistant's failures legible to the documentation team. An ungrounded-number rejection points at a specific chunk and a specific claim, which means your assistant's failure log is a list of pages where the content and the questions do not line up. Without grounding you have a pile of complaints; with it you have a prioritised content backlog. That reframes the work from AI safety into documentation prioritisation, and it is the framing a documentation lead will actually fund.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. The checker, plus a set of answers it rejects, including one that looks correct. The fused example is the one to show.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run the golden path. Five answers, four of which look right, three rejected for three different reasons. The number check passing "16" and rejecting "24" at 88% overlap is the moment that lands.
    2. Run it against real output. Take thirty answers from any documentation assistant you can query, and check them. Count the fused claims. That number is a finding nobody has.
    3. Put it in CI on a golden set. The checker plus twenty question-and-expected-source pairs, failing the build when the grounded rate drops. That is the step from a script to a gate.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Saying "we require citations" is near-universal and means almost nothing. A checker that rejects a fused claim carrying a valid citation, with the threshold justified from a labelled set, demonstrates that you know citations are not grounding.
