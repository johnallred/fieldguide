    Cognitive load in technical content¶

    The mean sentence length is fine on almost every corpus; the per-page maximum and the unprepared cross-reference are what actually cost the reader.

    core · difficulty 2 · 3h

    Adjacent, not quoted

    Neither posting names it. It is the mechanism behind every claim about reaching first success in minutes.

    Prerequisites: Instructional design for developers

    What it is¶

    The working-memory cost of following a page. The useful split is into three parts. The intrinsic difficulty of the subject, which you cannot reduce without teaching something easier. The extraneous cost added by the presentation, which is the only part a writer controls. And the germane effort the reader spends actually building a model, which is the part you want them to have capacity left for.

    Nearly every documentation complaint that gets reported as "too complicated" is extraneous load, and nearly every attempt to fix it by adding explanation makes it worse.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Because "a developer reaches a working result in fifteen minutes" is a claim about working memory, not about word count.

    A reader following a quickstart is holding several things at once: what they are trying to achieve, where they are in the sequence, what the last command printed, which of three similar-looking names they are supposed to use, and whether the thing that just appeared on screen is a problem. Every item the page adds to that stack is taken from the capacity they need for the actual subject. Exceed it and they do not report confusion, they close the tab, which is why this shows up in time to first success as a drop-off rather than as feedback.

    The golden path¶

    Measure load on a real corpus, discover that the summary statistic is useless, and find a worse problem the summary statistic hides. About thirty minutes.

    Step 1: the obvious measurement

    sents = []
for c in complete:
    prose = prose_of((docs / c.page).read_text())
    sents += [len(s.split()) for s in SENT.split(prose) if len(s.split()) >= 3]

    sentences: 4847   mean 19.6 words
longest sentence per page: mean 50   max 103 (tutorial-design)

    (Steps 1 and 2 were run when the corpus was 82 pages. They are kept as the pair that exposed the bug; every figure from step 3 onward is the finished 106-page corpus.)

    A 103-word sentence would be remarkable. Look at it:

    103 | The architectural ordering, eight steps, output only at the end:
      mkdir sync && cd sync python3 -m venv .venv && source .venv/bin/activate
      pip install widget click mkdir src tests cat > src/config.py <<'EOF' ...

    That is a shell session. The prose extractor had re-implemented fence detection as a toggle, and the page opens a four-backtick fence containing heredocs, so the toggle closed early and treated the commands as a sentence.

    The repository already contained a correct fence parser, written precisely because this problem is harder than it looks. The audit did not use it.

    Step 2: the same measurement, using the parser that already existed

    from fieldguide.fences import fence_spans, heredoc_body_lines
lines = text.splitlines()
skip  = heredoc_body_lines(lines)
incode = {n for sp in fence_spans(lines, skip=skip)
            for n in range(sp.open_line, sp.close_line + 1)}

    sentences: 5341   mean 16.7 words   median 15
longest sentence per page: mean 46   max 66 (reading-code-to-verify)

    Every figure moved, and the sentence count went up by 494, because the broken version had been swallowing real prose into pseudo-sentences as well as inventing them. An audit that re-implements a solved problem inherits exactly the bug the solution exists for.

    Step 3: the summary statistic is fine and it is useless

    The original run, when the corpus held 7,178 sentences rather than today's 8,541:

    mean 17.1 words, median 15
sentences over 45 words: 95 of 7178 (1.3%)

    By any style guide that is a healthy corpus. Now count pages rather than sentences:

    pages whose longest sentence exceeds 35 words: 100/106
pages whose longest sentence exceeds 45 words:  59/106
pages whose longest sentence exceeds 60 words:   6/106

    One and a third per cent of the sentences, and more than half the pages. Those two rows describe the same data, and only the second one describes the reader's experience, because a reader does not meet a corpus average. They meet one page, and on 59 of 106 pages they meet a sentence they have to read twice.

    The actionable metric is the per-page maximum. A mean is a measurement of the corpus; a maximum is a measurement of the worst moment a reader has, and load failures are worst-moment failures.

    Step 3a: part of that number was the splitter

    Acting on the figures above turned up a defect in the thing that produced them, which by now is the expected order of events in this corpus.

    The sentence boundary was a terminator followed by an optional closing quote or bracket, then whitespace. This corpus ends dozens of sentences in bold, so .** was not a boundary and every bolded sentence was silently joined to the one after it. Three of the six "sentences over 60 words" were two sentences with a .** between them, and the over-45 sentence count fell from 111 to 56 the moment the class was widened to include markup.

                                      before   after the fix   after editing
sentences over 45 words              111             56              33
pages whose longest exceeds 45        59             43              27
pages whose longest exceeds 60         6              5               0
longest sentence in the corpus        71             71              50

    The 111 in that first column and the 95 above it are the same measurement at two different times: the corpus kept growing between them. The page counts are the stable comparison, which is the argument for the per-page maximum made twice over.

    The editing pass is real work and it moved the middle column to the right one. The splitter fix moved the left column to the middle without touching a word of prose. Both are in the table because publishing only the second would make the editing look twice as effective as it was.

    Step 3b: the metric has a hole, and measuring it closed it

    A list item is not a sentence, so the prose measurement excludes list items. That exclusion is also a loophole: a 70-word sentence becomes compliant the moment somebody puts a hyphen in front of it, and the reader is no better off unless the content was genuinely a list.

    Four of the five worst sentences here were genuinely lists, and converting them to bullets is the right edit for a reader. It is also an edit that removes them from the metric rather than improving them, and those two facts are independent. So fieldguide audit now reports the longest sentence inside list items alongside the prose figure:

        in list items: longest mean 21, max 34
                   45+ on   0/106 pages

    No list item in this corpus hides a long sentence, which is worth knowing rather than assuming. The first version of that measurement counted each item's total words and reported an 81-word item, which was four short sentences in a proof task and not a readability problem at all. Sentences inside items are the comparable unit; item length is not.

    Step 4: the cost nobody measures

    Sentence length is the cheap half. Count what a page asks the reader to hold by way of cross-references:

    distinct concepts linked per page: mean 6.3   max 15 (systems-mindset)   min 4

    Then ask whether those links point at anything the reader has been prepared for. For each learning path, classify every link on its pages as backward (already read), forward (later in this path), or off-path:

      first-week           backward   9  forward  14  off-path  52
  content-architect    backward  15  forward  18  off-path  45
  docs-engineer        backward  34  forward  35  off-path  39
  knowledgeops         backward  44  forward  38  off-path  49
  developer-educator   backward  22  forward  27  off-path  32
  writer-who-builds    backward   4  forward   8  off-path  60
  prove-it             backward  22  forward  20  off-path  55
  prove-the-outcomes   backward  15  forward  15  off-path  32
  domain-context       backward   9  forward   7  off-path   8

    backward 174   forward 182   off-path 372
links pointing at material the reader has not read: 76%

    Nine paths, where this measurement first ran over seven. Two paths were added after it, and the per-path rows moved as well as the totals, because pages gained cross-references. The 76 per cent did not move at all, which is the useful thing about a ratio: it is the shape of the corpus rather than its size, and it is why that figure is the one quoted elsewhere.

    On first-week, a reader meets 75 concept links and nine of them point at something they have already read. The other 66 are an invitation to leave, each one carrying a small decision: is this a prerequisite I am missing, or an aside?

    That decision is pure extraneous load, and it is invisible to every readability measure. The corpus reads well sentence by sentence and asks its readers to make sixty-six unnecessary judgments per path.

    Step 5: what to do about each half

    The sentence half is a straightforward editing job against a per-page maximum. The six pages over 60 words took an afternoon, and there are now none.

    The link half is not fixed by linking less. A guide whose pages do not cross-reference is a worse guide. It is fixed by making the kind of link visible, so the reader's decision is made for them. A prerequisites line is a link you must follow. A "related" list at the foot is a link you may ignore. An inline link is neither, which is why the inline ones generate the load. This corpus has the first and the last and uses inline links for both jobs, which is the finding.

    What you built. A load measurement that uses the corpus's own fence parser, a per-page maximum that replaced a useless mean, and a cross-reference census showing 78 per cent of links point at unread material.

    Reference¶

    The three loads, and what moves each one¶

    Load Source Lever
    Intrinsic The subject's real difficulty Teach a smaller subject first
    Extraneous Presentation Everything on this page
    Germane Model-building Protect capacity for it

    The common mistake is attacking intrinsic load by adding explanation, which raises extraneous load and leaves less room for germane effort. The reader reports the same confusion about a longer page.

    Extraneous load, by source¶

    Source Shows up as Measured by
    Long sentences Re-reading Per-page maximum, not the mean
    Unprepared cross-references A decision per link The backward/forward/off-path census
    Split attention Scrolling between code and its explanation Code and prose adjacency
    Near-identical names Using the wrong one A terminology inventory
    Unstated prerequisites Failure three steps later Cold-start testing
    Options presented before a default Paralysis at step 1 Counting choices before first success
    Output not shown Uncertainty whether it worked Expected output per command

    The last two rows are the highest-yield fixes in most quickstarts and neither is a writing problem.

    Why the mean misleads¶

    Statistic Describes Useful for
    Mean sentence length The corpus Reporting
    Median The corpus Reporting
    Per-page maximum The reader's worst moment Editing
    Count of pages over a threshold The size of the job Planning
    Percentage of sentences over it Nothing a reader experiences Reassuring yourself

    Load failures are worst-moment failures, so every statistic worth acting on is a maximum or a count of pages, never a central tendency.

    Chunking, and its limit¶

    Breaking a long procedure into named steps genuinely reduces load, because the reader can discard the contents of a completed step and keep only its name. That is why golden paths in this guide are numbered and named.

    The limit is that chunking only helps when the chunks are independent. Steps that require holding state from three steps back are not chunked, they are merely numbered, and the numbering disguises the problem by making the page look organised.

    What load is not an argument for¶

    Not Because
    Shorter documentation A page can be long and low-load
    Removing detail Detail the reader needs is intrinsic
    Simplified vocabulary The real terms are what they will search for
    Fewer links The fix is marking their kind, not deleting them
    One page per idea Fragmentation adds navigation load

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with the three-way split, briefly. "Intrinsic load is the subject, extraneous load is the presentation, germane load is the reader actually building a model. Only the middle one is mine, and most attempts to reduce the first by adding explanation increase the second. 'Fifteen minutes to a working result' is a claim about working memory, not about word count."

    The follow-up is how you measure it, and this is where to bring the numbers. Mean sentence length on my corpus is 15.6 words, which is healthy and useless. A reader never meets an average, so the metric I act on is the per-page maximum: 59 of 106 pages carried a sentence over 45 words and six carried one over 60. After an editing pass, 27 pages and none. The honest part of that story is that fixing the sentence splitter accounted for as much of the improvement as the editing did, because it had never treated a sentence ending in bold as a sentence ending.

    The stronger half, if there is time for one more thing. I classified every cross-reference by whether the reader had reached it yet, and 76 per cent of links point at material they have not read. On the shortest path, nine links of 75 point backwards. Each of the other 66 is a decision about whether something is a missing prerequisite or an aside, which is invisible to every readability score. The fix is not fewer links, it is making the kind of link visible, because a prerequisites line and a "related" footer make the decision for the reader and an inline link does not.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Load is a model, not a measurement, and the numbers above are proxies for something nobody can observe directly. A page with a 60-word sentence may be perfectly clear and a page of short sentences may be unreadable. The measurements earn their place by finding candidates quickly, and the reading still has to happen; treating any of them as a gate produces staccato prose that scores well.

    One sharper point. My own load audit was wrong, and wrong in the way this whole area is wrong: it re-implemented fence detection as a toggle, so a page with four-backtick fences and heredocs leaked a shell session into the prose and reported a 103-word sentence. The repository already contained a correct fence parser. The corrected run moved every figure and found 494 more sentences, because the broken version had been merging real prose too. An audit that re-implements a solved problem inherits the exact bug the solution exists for.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A per-page maximum sentence length and a cross-reference census over a corpus you own, both computed with that corpus's own parser.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Measure the per-page maximum sentence length on your docs, then read the three worst sentences out loud. The measurement takes five minutes and the reading is what convinces anybody.
    2. Take your shortest learning path and classify every link on its pages as backward, forward or off-path. The ratio will be worse than you expect.
    3. Open your quickstart and count the choices it presents before the reader's first success. Each one is extraneous load at the worst possible moment.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Every figure in steps 2 through 4 comes from fieldguide audit, a committed subcommand rather than a script that was thrown away, so a reader can run it and get these numbers. fences.py is the parser it uses, and it exists because the naive version was wrong three times; tests/test_audit.py holds a fixture for each of those three failures, including the nested fence that leaked a shell session into the prose.

    What the guide does not have is any evidence that lowering these numbers improved comprehension for a real reader, which would need assessment and a population. The demonstrated page records this as partial on exactly that split: the instrument is demonstrated, the effect is not.
