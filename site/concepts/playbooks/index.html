    Playbooks and decision guides¶

    Turning what one person knows into a branching path other people can follow, and validating that every branch ends.

    core · difficulty 2 · 4h

    Asked for by name

    NVIDIA: "Capture tribal knowledge from engineering, product, and operations, and turn it into playbooks, decision guides, and self-service paths"

    Prerequisites: Interviewing subject-matter experts

    What it is¶

    A page that branches. A tutorial takes one reader down one path; a playbook asks what situation you are in and routes you accordingly. It is the right shape for troubleshooting, for incident response, and for any question whose honest answer is "it depends", because it makes the depends explicit.

    The move that turns it from a document into infrastructure is writing the branches as data rather than as prose. Then you can render the page and validate the structure, which is the difference between a playbook and a wiki page with a lot of nested bullets.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    NVIDIA's bullet is the clearest statement of leverage anywhere in either posting: capture what individuals know and turn it into self-service paths, so fewer people have to ask "how do I…?".

    The reason this needs a page of its own rather than being a kind of how-to is that tribal knowledge is conditional. The expert does not know one procedure; they know how to tell which of six situations they are in, and the discriminating questions are the knowledge. Writing a linear how-to for each branch loses the part that made the expert valuable, which is the routing.

    It is also the output of SME interviews, which is why that is the prerequisite rather than information architecture. You cannot write the branches without having asked somebody "how do you know which one it is?"

    The golden path¶

    Write a decision guide as data, then validate its structure. About thirty-five minutes.

    Committed as drills/playbook.py, which is the complete version of what follows: this page prints the playbook in full and the validator in fragments, so the output below used to come from a check_playbook.py that appears nowhere. A test asserts the drill still prints what this page quotes, and that the playbook it validates is the one below.

    Step 1: the branches, as data

    The subject is a real one: an integration has failed and you have to work out why. This is the shape of knowledge a Boomi or ETL engineer carries around in their head.

    mkdir -p playbook-demo && cd playbook-demo
cat > playbook.yaml <<'EOF'
name: Integration is failing
entry: symptom
nodes:
  symptom:
    ask: What does the failure look like?
    options:
      - {answer: No data arrived at all, go: check-trigger}
      - {answer: Some records missing, go: check-filter}
      - {answer: Wrong values in the data, go: check-mapping}
  check-trigger:
    ask: Did the schedule fire?
    options:
      - {answer: "No", do: fix-schedule}
      - {answer: "Yes", go: check-auth}
  check-auth:
    ask: Are the credentials current?
    options:
      - {answer: "No", do: rotate-credentials}
      - {answer: "Yes", do: escalate-platform}
  check-filter:
    ask: Does the filter reference a field that was renamed?
    options:
      - {answer: "Yes", do: fix-filter}
      - {answer: "No", do: escalate-platform}
  check-mapping:
    ask: Is a dynamic process property set where you expect?
    options:
      - {answer: "No", do: trace-dpp}
      - {answer: "Yes", do: escalate-platform}
outcomes:
  fix-schedule: Re-enable the schedule and note why it was off.
  rotate-credentials: Rotate the token, then re-run the failed window.
  fix-filter: Update the filter to the current field name.
  trace-dpp: Find every get and set of the property, then correct the source.
  escalate-platform: Hand to the platform team with the trace attached.
  orphaned-outcome: Nothing routes here.
EOF

    Note the shape. Every node is a question with discriminating answers, and every path terminates in an outcome that says what to do. escalate-platform appears three times, which is correct: "I have ruled out what I can" is a legitimate destination and a playbook that pretends otherwise sends people in circles.

    Step 2: the five things that can be wrong with it

    # every `go` and `do` target resolves, and no option goes nowhere
for nid, node in nodes.items():
    for opt in node["options"]:
        if "go" in opt and opt["go"] not in nodes:
            problems.append(f"{nid}: 'go: {opt['go']}' is not a node")
        if "do" in opt and opt["do"] not in outcomes:
            problems.append(f"{nid}: 'do: {opt['do']}' is not an outcome")
        if "go" not in opt and "do" not in opt:
            problems.append(f"{nid}: option {opt['answer']!r} goes nowhere")

# every outcome is reachable
reached = {o["do"] for n in nodes.values() for o in n["options"] if "do" in o}
for oid in outcomes:
    if oid not in reached:
        problems.append(f"outcome {oid!r}: nothing routes here")

# every node is reachable from the entry
reachable, frontier = {entry}, [entry]
while frontier:
    for opt in nodes[frontier.pop()]["options"]:
        if opt.get("go") in nodes and opt["go"] not in reachable:
            reachable.add(opt["go"])
            frontier.append(opt["go"])
for nid in nodes:
    if nid not in reachable:
        problems.append(f"node {nid!r}: unreachable from {entry!r}")

# no cycles, because a decision guide that loops never ends

    The node-reachability check is the same defect as the outcome one, a level up, and it is the likelier of the two in a guide that has been edited for a year. A question nobody can reach is a branch of the expert's knowledge that has quietly left the playbook, and it is harder to notice by reading than an orphaned outcome, because an unreachable node is perfectly well-formed on its own.

    Step 3: run it

    python3 check_playbook.py

    PLAYBOOK  outcome 'orphaned-outcome': nothing routes here
FAIL: 5 nodes, 6 outcomes, 1 problem(s)

    One finding, which is the deliberately planted orphan. Remove it:

    ok: 5 nodes, 5 outcomes, 0 problem(s)

    Step 4: confirm each defect class is caught

    Inject the other four, one at a time, and print every finding each produces:

      dangling-go  a target that does not resolve
    check-trigger: 'go: check-athu' is not a node
    node 'check-auth': unreachable from 'symptom'
  cycle        a path that never ends
    cycle: symptom -> check-trigger -> check-auth -> symptom
  dead-end     an option with no next step
    check-trigger: option 'No' goes nowhere
    outcome 'fix-schedule': nothing routes here
  unreachable  a question nobody can get to
    node 'check-retry': unreachable from 'symptom'

    Five classes, five catches. Each is a specific way a prose playbook fails silently:

    Defect What a reader experiences
    Orphaned outcome Advice nobody can reach; usually the most interesting advice
    Unreachable node A question that has silently left the guide, taking its branch with it
    Dangling target "See the authentication section", which does not exist
    Cycle Being sent back to a question already answered
    Dead-end option An answer with no next step, so the reader is stuck

    Every one of those exists in prose troubleshooting guides and none is detectable by reading, because reading follows one path at a time and these are properties of the whole graph.

    A findings count is not a defect count¶

    Two of those four injections report two findings, and both second findings are consequences of the first.

    Removing the do from check-trigger's first option leaves that option dangling and orphans fix-schedule, because that option was the only route to it. Mistyping the go target breaks that edge and strands check-auth, which nothing else routes to.

    So four injected defects produce six findings, and two of the six vanish when the other four are fixed. That matters the moment anybody plans work from the number: a graph validator reports symptoms, and in a graph one broken edge produces as many symptoms as there are properties that depended on it. The repair list is shorter than the findings list, always, and the only way to know how much shorter is to fix one and re-run.

    This page used to show a single line per class, which hid both cascades. The block above is what the validator actually prints.

    What you built. A decision guide whose structure is checkable, the knowledge that the cycle in step 4 is the failure readers complain about without being able to name, and a worked reason to distrust findings counts.

    Reference¶

    When a playbook is the right shape¶

    Use a playbook Use something else
    The answer depends on observable conditions One procedure, one audience: a how-to
    An expert routes before acting The task is linear
    Troubleshooting, triage, incident response Explaining why something works: an explanation
    "It depends" is the honest answer There is a single best path: a golden path
    Several paths converge on an escalation Nobody ever needs to escalate

    The questions that make it work¶

    A playbook's quality is in its discriminating questions, and the test for each one is whether a reader can answer it without already knowing the diagnosis.

    Good question Bad question
    "Did the schedule fire?" "Is the trigger misconfigured?"
    "Are the credentials current?" "Is this an auth problem?"
    "Is the property set where you expect?" "Is the mapping wrong?"

    The bad column asks the reader to make the determination the playbook exists to help them make. This is the single most common defect in hand-written troubleshooting guides, and no validator catches it: it needs a reader who does not already know the answer.

    Getting it out of somebody's head¶

    Technique Produces
    Watch them do it, and write down the questions they ask The branch structure
    Ask "how do you know it is that and not the other thing?" The discriminating questions
    Ask for the last five times they were wrong The branches a confident expert omits
    Ask what they check first and why The entry node
    Ask when they give up and escalate The terminal outcomes

    The third row is the one that improves a playbook most. Experts narrate their successful path and omit the branches they have learned to skip, and those skipped branches are exactly where a newcomer gets lost.

    Playbook as data, and what it buys¶

    Output From the same file
    A rendered page Generated, so prose and structure cannot disagree
    Structural validation The four checks above
    An interactive widget Optional; the data supports it
    An agent skill A model can walk the graph rather than guess
    Coverage measurement Which outcomes fire most, from incident records

    The last two are why this is worth the extra work. A playbook as prose is a page; a playbook as data is something an assistant can traverse, and the routing is the part an assistant is otherwise worst at.

    Keeping one honest¶

    Rule Why
    Every path terminates Checked above
    Escalation is a real outcome Pretending otherwise sends people round again
    Questions are observable, not diagnostic See the table above
    Review it after an incident it failed to cover The missing branch is now known
    Date the outcomes "Rotate the token" changes when the auth system does

    How to talk about it¶

    Lead with the routing. "Tribal knowledge is conditional. The expert does not know one procedure, they know how to tell which of six situations they are in, and the discriminating questions are the actual knowledge. A linear how-to per branch loses exactly that, which is why these are decision guides and not tutorials."

    The follow-up is how you keep one correct, and the answer is to write the branches as data and validate the graph: no dangling targets, no cycles, no dead-end options, no unreachable outcomes. Four checks that catch failures no amount of reading will, because reading follows one path and these are properties of the whole structure.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. A playbook as data costs you a generator and a validator, and it makes casual editing harder: a contributor who wants to add a sentence now has to understand a schema. On a guide with four branches that is not worth it, and prose with a diagram is the better answer. It starts paying when the graph is big enough that nobody can hold it in their head, which is roughly the point at which it also stops being maintainable as prose.

    One sharper point. The quality of a playbook is entirely in whether its questions can be answered by someone who does not already know the answer. "Is the trigger misconfigured?" is useless and extremely common; "Did the schedule fire?" is useful. No validator catches that, which is why these need testing with a reader who is genuinely stuck rather than review by the expert who wrote them.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A playbook for something you actually know, as data, with the four structural checks and a rendered page from the same source.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run the golden path. Step 4 is worth doing in full, because the cycle finding is the reader complaint you have probably received without recognising it.
    2. Take the thing colleagues ask you most often and write its branches. Not the procedure, the questions.
    3. Give it to someone who does not know the answer and watch where they stop. That is the diagnostic-question defect, and it is the only way to find it.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Neither of this guide's repositories contains a playbook, which the demonstrated page records. The validator above is real and runs, and the honest limit is that it checks structure only: it proves every path ends somewhere, and it cannot tell you whether the questions are answerable. Being clear about which half a tool covers is the point.
