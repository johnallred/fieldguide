    Technical writing as a craft¶

    Writing that a competent stranger can act on without asking you a question.

    core · difficulty 2 · 8h

    Strong technical writing and information architecture skills for developer documentation

    Source: intel posting, verbatim

    Leads to: Giving architecture owners implementation feedback

    What it is¶

    Writing that a competent stranger can act on without asking you a question. The test is behavioural rather than aesthetic: not whether the prose reads well, but whether the reader does the right thing next.

    It is a craft in the specific sense that it improves with deliberate practice on feedback, and that people who are good at it can say why a sentence is wrong.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    Both postings list it as a minimum qualification. Intel asks for "strong technical writing" in the same breath as information architecture; NVIDIA opens with "You're a writer who builds" and wants eight years in technical writing or developer documentation.

    This guide's first version covered pipelines, retrieval, taxonomy and validators and had no page for writing well. That was its worst gap, and the reason it was easy to fall into is worth naming: everything else in this field is more legible. A chunker either works or does not. A link checker either fails the build or does not. Prose quality has no test suite, so it quietly becomes the thing nobody budgets for while everyone agrees it matters.

    On a Tuesday this is less glamorous than the postings suggest. An engineer hands you four paragraphs about a new flag. The paragraphs are accurate. They also open with implementation history, use three names for the same thing, bury the one command a reader needs in the middle of the third sentence, and never say what happens if you get it wrong. Turning that into a page someone can act on, in twenty minutes, without losing anything true, is the job. You will do it several times a day.

    The second reason it is core: in a role where you build the validators, your own writing is the reference implementation. A style rule you cannot follow is a style rule the team will litigate.

    The golden path¶

    No installation, which makes this the one golden path in the guide that is pure practice. About thirty minutes, and it still ends in a measurement.

    Step 1: get a page that is accurate and unusable

    mkdir -p writing-drill && cd writing-drill
cat > before.md <<'EOF'
# Configuring Retry Behaviour

Historically the widget client did not retry at all, which caused problems
during the 2024 migration when the upstream service was being restarted
frequently. We subsequently added retry logic, and after some discussion
about whether the backoff should be linear or exponential, exponential was
chosen. The retry configuration is controlled through the client
configuration object, which also controls other things like timeouts, and
users should be aware that the retry count does not include the initial
attempt, so a retry count of 3 means 4 total requests will be made. It is
generally recommended that you do not set the retry count very high because
this can cause the client to block for a long time, and in some cases it may
be better to fail fast and let the caller decide. The maximum is 10.
EOF
wc -w before.md

    Nothing in that paragraph is false. A reader who needs to set a retry count has to read 150 words and do arithmetic.

    Step 2: four passes, in this order

    Order matters: reorganising sentences you are about to delete is wasted work.

    cat > passes.md <<'EOF'
1. CUT      Remove what the reader does not need in order to act. History,
            decision records and hedging all go. Target: half the words.
2. ANSWER   Put the thing they came for in the first sentence.
3. NAME     One term per concept, every time. No synonyms for variety.
4. FAIL     Say what goes wrong, and what to do about it.
EOF
cat passes.md

    Step 3: do the passes

    cat > after.md <<'EOF'
# Configure retry behaviour

Set `retries` on the client. The default is 3.

    client = WidgetClient(retries=3)

`retries` counts retries, not attempts: `retries=3` makes up to 4 requests.
The maximum is 10.

## Choosing a value

| Situation | Value |
|---|---|
| A caller is waiting on the response | 1 to 2 |
| Background job that can afford to wait | 3 to 5 |
| Upstream is known to restart often | up to 10 |

Retries use exponential backoff, so a high count blocks for a long time. If the
caller handles failure better than the client can wait, prefer failing fast
over raising this.

## Why exponential backoff

See the retry design note.
EOF
echo "before: $(wc -w < before.md) words"
echo "after:  $(wc -w < after.md) words"

    Step 4: measure the thing that matters

    Word count is a proxy. The real test is how far a reader travels before they can act.

    python3 - <<'PY'
import pathlib

def words_before_the_answer(path, marker="retries"):
    text = pathlib.Path(path).read_text()
    index = text.find(marker)
    return len(text[:index].split())

for name in ("before.md", "after.md"):
    print(f"{name:<12} {words_before_the_answer(name):>3} words before the answer")
PY

    What you built. The same facts, with the reader reaching the answer in a fraction of the distance, plus two things the original lacked entirely: a table for choosing a value, and a statement of what goes wrong.

    Note that the rewrite is not merely shorter. The history moved to an explanation page rather than being destroyed, which is Diátaxis doing the work. Keep both files: a before-and-after you can show is the most persuasive artifact in this whole category.

    Reference¶

    The four passes¶

    Pass Question Typical cut
    Cut Does the reader need this to act? Implementation history, decision narrative, hedging, apologies
    Answer Is the thing they came for first? Move the command or the value to sentence one
    Name One term per concept? Synonyms introduced for variety
    Fail What goes wrong, and then what? Usually missing entirely

    Sentence-level habits that carry most of the weight¶

    Habit Instead of Write
    Active voice for instructions "The flag should be set" "Set the flag"
    Second person "Users can configure" "Configure"
    Present tense "The client will retry" "The client retries"
    Condition first "Run drain if deploying a schema change" "Before a schema change, run drain"
    Say the number "A reasonably high value" "Up to 10"
    One idea per sentence Three clauses and a however Three sentences

    Condition-first is the least obvious and the most valuable. A reader scanning for their situation needs to know whether a sentence applies to them before they read it.

    Words that signal a problem¶

    Word What it usually hides
    "simply", "just", "easy" A step the author stopped seeing
    "should" Nobody checked whether it does
    "note that", "it is important to" Something belonging earlier, or in a callout
    "etc.", "and so on" The author did not finish the list
    "robust", "seamless", "powerful" Nothing. Delete
    "obviously", "of course" The reader is about to feel stupid

    These are exactly what a Vale rule catches, which is the link between this page and the automation half of the job.

    What technical writing is not¶

    Not Difference
    Good prose Literary quality is irrelevant; actionability is not
    Brevity A reference page should be exhaustive. Short is a means
    Simplification Never make it less true to make it shorter
    Explaining everything An explanation inside a how-to guide is a defect

    How to talk about it¶

    What a competent answer sounds like. Do not call your writing clear and concise; everyone does. Describe a specific edit and its cost. "The page documented a flag accurately in 150 words of history. I moved the history to an explanation page, put the default in the first sentence, added a table for choosing a value and a line on what happens if you set it too high. The reader reaches the answer in one sentence instead of five, and nothing true was lost."

    The follow-up is usually "how do you get engineers to write documentation". The answer that lands is that mostly you do not, and designing around that is the skill. You take their accurate, badly organised paragraph as a source rather than a draft, and you ask them to review for accuracy rather than for style. An engineer will happily confirm a number is right. They will not happily restructure a page.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Editing someone else's writing hard enough to make it usable costs political capital every time, and the cost is highest with the people whose expertise you most need. There is a real version of this job where you keep every contributor happy and the documentation stays bad. The mitigation is to move standards into linters and templates, so the rule is impersonal and the argument happens once against the rule rather than weekly against a person. That is the systems mindset applied to a social problem.

    One sharper point. The hardest part of technical writing is not sentences, it is knowing what the reader does not know, and it is hard because writing a page destroys your ability to judge it. Once you understand the system, the gap you left is invisible to you. Nothing fixes that from the inside, which is the real argument for cold-start testing and for watching someone new follow your page without helping them. Saying that connects the craft to the automation, which is the whole pitch of both roles.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A before-and-after, with the reasoning. Not a writing sample: a sample shows you can write, which is table stakes. An edit shows you can diagnose.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run the golden path on a real page. Find a genuinely bad page in a project you know, rewrite it with the four passes, and write three sentences on what each pass removed. Twenty minutes, and the highest-value portfolio item on this page.
    2. Encode one of your own rules as a linter. Take a habit you care about, hedge words or synonym drift, and write the Vale rule. A writer who ships their style guide as code is making precisely the argument both postings are hiring for.
    3. Watch someone follow one of your pages without helping them. Note where they stop, then fix the page rather than explaining it. That note is more convincing about your judgement than any finished page. Predict where they will stop before you watch. Being wrong about that is the point of doing it with a person rather than from the page.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Anyone can produce a polished page. The signal is being able to say what is wrong with a page and in what order you would fix it, and having an example where you were the one who was wrong. If the "before" is always someone else's writing, that is a smaller claim than one where it is yours.

    How this would be demonstrated elsewhere¶

    This one cannot be demonstrated by a repository anywhere, not for want of tooling. A repository can show that a page exists, conforms and is accurate; it cannot show that a reader understood it, and understanding is the claim.

    Where the skill is actually exercised. In an edit of someone else's draft, where the measure is whether the author accepts the change and can say why. In the gap between a first draft and a third. In the decision to cut a section that took a day to write.

    What a learner would do. Take a page you did not write and that works, and rewrite it badly on purpose: bury the first instruction under motivation, move the expected output away from the command, replace the reader's word with the precise term everywhere. Then read both aloud. The exercise is instructive in the direction people do not try, because the defects you introduce deliberately are the ones you stop introducing accidentally.

    Then the reverse: take a page that does not work, and fix it without adding words. The constraint is the whole exercise. Most bad documentation is bad by addition, and the instinct to explain more is the instinct to resist.

    What competence looks like, in evidence someone else can see.

    Evidence Why it carries weight
    A diff that shortens a page and improves it The rarest and most convincing artifact
    An author thanking you for a cut They had to agree, which is the test
    A page whose support questions stopped Indirect, and the only outcome measure
    A style decision you reversed, with the reason Shows the judgment is live rather than inherited
    Writing that survives a hostile review Review is the nearest thing to a reader

    Why none of that commits to a repository. The diff is in someone else's project. The acknowledgement is in a review thread. The support question that stopped is an absence, and absences do not appear in version control. This is the honest reason this concept has no demonstration here and would have none anywhere: the evidence exists, it is just not the kind of thing you can clone.

    What a corpus can show is the mechanisable part, which is why this guide enforces a page contract, a duration, a stated result and a verification section. Those are the floor. The craft is what happens above it, and the distance between a page that satisfies the contract and a page that works is the whole subject.
