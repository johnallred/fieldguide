    Extracting tribal knowledge¶

    Getting what an expert knows out of their head and into something other people can use without them.

    core · difficulty 2 · 3h

    Turning documentation, standards, and tribal knowledge into tooling, templates, and self-service paths

    Source: nvidia posting, verbatim

    Leads to: Playbooks and decision guides, Working with engineers as sources

    What it is¶

    Getting what an expert knows out of their head and into a form other people can use without them. Tribal knowledge is the subset of what a team knows that has never been written down, usually because the people who have it do not experience it as knowledge. It feels like common sense.

    The work is an interview technique plus a judgement about what shape the result should take, and the second part is where most of the value is.

    Why the job asks for it¶

    NVIDIA names it twice. Once in the framing: "turning documentation, standards, and tribal knowledge into tooling, templates, and self-service paths, so fewer people have to ask how do I". Once as a responsibility: "Capture tribal knowledge from engineering, product, and operations, and turn it into playbooks, decision guides, and self-service paths".

    Notice what the sentences have in common. Neither says "write it down". Both specify an output form, and none of the forms is a page. That is the distinctive thing about this role, and the thing most technical writers get wrong. The deliverable for tribal knowledge is often a template, a validator, or a decision tree. The knowledge in question is usually a judgement rather than a fact, and a page describing a judgement gets read once and forgotten.

    On a Tuesday it looks like this. A staff engineer is the only person who knows which of four ways to configure a simulation is right for a given workload. Three teams ask her every quarter. The naive output is a page titled "Configuration options", which already exists and which nobody uses because it lists the options without ranking them. The useful output is a decision guide: four questions in order, each narrowing the choice, ending in one answer and the reason. That is the same knowledge and a different artifact, and only one of them stops the questions.

    The second reason this matters: the knowledge is the thing the role is protecting against. A platform whose operational knowledge lives in four people's heads is one reorganisation away from losing it.

    The golden path¶

    Run one extraction, on a real person, and produce something that is not a page. Forty-five minutes including the conversation.

    Step 1: find the question, not the topic

    Topics produce overviews. Questions produce usable artifacts.

    mkdir -p tribal-drill && cd tribal-drill
cat > scope.md <<'EOF'
BAD SCOPE      "Tell me about our deployment process."
               Produces a 2,000-word overview nobody reads.

GOOD SCOPE     "What do you get asked about deployment that you wish
               people could answer themselves?"
               Produces the three things that actually recur.

THE PIVOT      Do not ask what they know. Ask what they get interrupted
               about. Interruptions are the measurable shadow of tribal
               knowledge, and the expert can list them instantly.
EOF
cat scope.md

    Step 2: run the interview with the five questions

    cat > interview.md <<'EOF'
# Extraction interview, 30 minutes

1. WHAT RECURS
   "What do people ask you more than once a month?"
   -> the list. Pick one. Do not try to cover all of it.

2. THE DECISION
   "When someone asks that, what do you need to know before you can
   answer?"
   -> these become the questions in the decision guide, in this order.

3. THE WRONG ANSWER
   "What do people do when they guess? What goes wrong?"
   -> the failure modes. This is the half that never gets written down
   and the half that makes the artifact trustworthy.

4. THE EXCEPTION
   "When is the usual answer wrong?"
   -> the thing that will make your first draft embarrassing if you
   skip it. Always ask it.

5. THE TELL
   "How do you know, looking at a request, which case it is?"
   -> the signal. Often something they have never articulated, and
   usually the single most valuable sentence in the interview.
EOF
cat interview.md

    Step 3: write down what you heard, in their words, before you shape it

    Do this before designing the artifact. Shaping while listening loses the exceptions.

    cat > raw.md <<'EOF'
# Raw notes: batch size for inference workloads  (SME: A. Patel, 30 min)

RECURS      "Which batch size should I use" -- several times a month,
            from three teams.

DECISION    Needs to know: is latency user-facing? how much GPU memory?
            is the model quantised? is the traffic bursty?

WRONG       People copy the number from the tutorial (32). On a latency-
            sensitive endpoint that adds ~200ms p99. On a small GPU it
            OOMs at load, not at startup, so it passes smoke tests.

EXCEPTION   Quantised models tolerate much larger batches; the memory
            math is different and the tutorial number is far too low.

TELL        "If they mention a p99 target, it is latency-sensitive and
            the answer is almost always 1 or 2, whatever the memory
            allows."
EOF
cat raw.md

    Step 4: choose the output form, then build it

    cat > choose.md <<'EOF'
Is the knowledge...        Then the artifact is...
-------------------------  ------------------------------------------
a fact                     a reference entry
a procedure                a how-to guide
a JUDGEMENT between        a DECISION GUIDE
  known options
a judgement that can be    a VALIDATOR or a default in the tooling
  computed from inputs
a sequence under pressure   a PLAYBOOK
a convention people        a TEMPLATE
  keep getting wrong
EOF
cat choose.md

    This one is a judgement between known options, so it is a decision guide.

    cat > decision-guide.md <<'EOF'
---
title: Choosing a batch size
summary: Four questions that determine the batch size for an inference workload.
diataxis: how-to
audience: developer
source: A. Patel, platform team, 2026-10
---

# Choosing a batch size

Do not copy the number from the tutorial. The tutorial uses 32 because it is
convenient for a demo, and it is wrong for most production workloads.

## 1. Is anything waiting on the response?

If a user or a caller is waiting, you are latency-sensitive. **Use 1 or 2.**

The tell: if you have a p99 target, the answer is almost always 1 or 2,
whatever memory allows. Batching at 32 on a latency-sensitive endpoint adds
roughly 200ms at p99.

If nothing is waiting, continue.

## 2. Is the model quantised?

If yes, the memory arithmetic is different and the tutorial's number is far
too low. Start at 64 and raise it while watching memory.

## 3. How much GPU memory do you have at peak, not at startup?

Size for peak. The common failure is an out-of-memory error under load rather
than at startup, which means it passes a smoke test and fails in production.

## 4. Is the traffic bursty?

Bursty traffic with a large batch size queues. Prefer a smaller batch and more
replicas.

## If you get this wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| p99 latency far above p50 | Batch size too high for a latency-sensitive path |
| OOM under load, fine at startup | Sized for startup memory, not peak |
| Throughput lower than expected on a quantised model | Batch size too low |
EOF
wc -l decision-guide.md

    What you built. A page that answers a recurring question in the order the expert actually thinks about it, including the failure modes and the exception, with the source and date recorded in the front matter.

    The source field matters more than it looks. Tribal knowledge has a shelf life, and a reader in two years needs to know who said this and when, so they can go and ask whether it is still true. That one line is the difference between a document and a captured claim.

    Reference¶

    The five questions, and what each one is for¶

    Question Produces
    What do people ask you more than once a month? Scope, chosen by frequency rather than by topic
    What do you need to know before you can answer? The decision guide's questions, in order
    What goes wrong when people guess? Failure modes, which nobody writes down
    When is the usual answer wrong? The exception that saves your draft
    How do you tell which case it is? The signal, usually never articulated

    Choosing the output form¶

    The knowledge is The artifact is
    A fact A reference entry
    A procedure A how-to guide
    A judgement between known options A decision guide
    A judgement computable from inputs A validator, or a better default in the tooling
    A sequence executed under pressure A playbook
    A convention people keep getting wrong A template
    A number that drifts A freshness check on the page

    The fourth row is the one that distinguishes this role. If the judgement can be computed, the right output is code, not prose. If the tutorial's batch size is wrong for most workloads, the strongest fix is changing the default and warning on the bad combination, and the page becomes an explanation of a safe default rather than a warning about a dangerous one.

    Interview mechanics that matter¶

    Do Why
    30 minutes, one question An hour on a topic produces an overview
    Record or take verbatim notes Their phrasing is usually better than yours, especially the tell
    Ask for the last time it happened Concrete incidents surface exceptions; general questions do not
    Write the draft, then have them correct it Experts review well and compose badly
    Send the draft within a day Their context decays fast
    Put their name and the date on it Captured knowledge needs provenance

    Failure modes¶

    Failure What it looks like
    Interviewing the topic A 2,000-word overview that answers nothing specific
    Capturing the answer, not the decision "Use 16" with no account of when that is wrong
    Skipping the exception A guide that is confidently wrong for quantised models
    Writing a page when the fix was a default The questions keep coming because nobody reads the page
    No provenance In two years nobody knows whether it is still true
    Interviewing one person You have captured one person's opinion as doctrine

    How to talk about it¶

    What a competent answer sounds like. Name the output form and why you chose it. "An engineer was being asked monthly which batch size to use. The page that existed listed the options without ranking them, so I built a decision guide with the four questions she actually asks, in order, plus the failure modes and the quantised-model exception. Then I argued for changing the tutorial's default, because the real problem was that the demo number was being copied into production."

    That last move is what separates this from note-taking.

    The follow-up is about experts who will not talk to you, or who talk and then never review the draft. The honest answer is that a thirty-minute meeting about one recurring question is an easy ask and a one-hour meeting about a topic is not, so scope is your main lever. Beyond that: write the draft wrong on purpose and send it, because experts who will not compose will happily correct, and a wrong draft generates a faster response than a request for information.

    The tradeoff to volunteer. Extraction takes the expert's time, and the people with the most tribal knowledge are the most interrupted people on the team. You are asking for the scarcest resource in the building to reduce demand on itself, which is a good trade and an unobvious one. The way to make it land is to lead with the interruption count rather than with documentation: "you get asked this three times a month, let me take thirty minutes and make it stop."

    One sharper point. Tribal knowledge is tribal for a reason, and the reason is usually that it is a judgement with exceptions, which is genuinely hard to write down and easy to write down badly. A confidently wrong decision guide is worse than no guide, because people follow it. This is why question four exists and why provenance is non-negotiable. Saying that demonstrates you understand why the knowledge was not already written down, which is a more sophisticated position than assuming nobody got round to it.

    How to prove it¶

    The artifact. A decision guide with a named source and a date, for a question that genuinely recurred. Not an overview.

    Three things to do, in order:

    1. Run one extraction on a real person. Thirty minutes, one recurring question, the five questions above. Produce something that is not a page if the knowledge is not a fact.
    2. Count the interruptions before and after. Ask the expert, a month later, whether the question stopped. That number is the whole argument, and it is what NVIDIA means by "measurable reductions in support load".
    3. Find one case where the answer was code. A judgement that could be a default, a warning, or a validator. Propose it. Being the documentation person who argues for changing a default rather than documenting it is memorable.

    What makes it credible rather than decorative. Everyone in this field says they interview subject matter experts. A decision guide with four ordered questions, an exception, a failure table and a named source is specific enough to be checked, and "she stopped getting asked" is the only outcome that matters.

    How this would be demonstrated elsewhere¶

    The artifact this produces is a page, and a page does not reveal how it was obtained. What would demonstrate the skill is the gap between what an expert said and what they knew, which only exists in the room.

    What you need. An engineer who knows something undocumented, thirty minutes, and a prepared set of questions. No tooling. The scarce resource is the engineer's attention, which is why the preparation is the skill.

    What a learner would do. Pick something in your own product that is undocumented because it is "obvious", and interview whoever knows it, using questions that cannot be answered abstractly:

    What breaks most often, and what does the person do first?
Walk me through the last time this went wrong.
What do you know that is not written down anywhere?
What does everyone get wrong when they first use this?
If I changed this, what would you be worried about?

    Then write the page, send it back, and count the corrections. The count is the measurement: a first draft from a good interview comes back with factual corrections, and one from a bad interview comes back rewritten.

    What competence looks like.

    Evidence Why it carries weight
    A page the expert approved with few edits The interview captured the right things
    A documented failure mode nobody had written You got tacit knowledge, not a restatement
    A second interview that was shorter You learned what to ask
    An expert who volunteers things afterwards The hardest signal to fake
    A question you stopped asking, and why The questions improve or they are a script

    Why it does not commit to a repository. The page commits; the interview does not. A reader cannot tell whether a well-documented failure mode came from an engineer, from reading the source, or from a support ticket, and those represent very different skills. The one piece of evidence that would distinguish them, the correction count on the first draft, lives in a review thread.

    The adjacent thing that is demonstrable. Reading the source to verify what you were told is a separate concept, reading code to verify, and the combination is what makes either trustworthy: an interview tells you where to look, and the code tells you whether the memory was right.
